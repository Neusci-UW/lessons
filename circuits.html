Simple circuits: reflexes and lateral inhibition

Most lessons follow one neuron or one synapse. Here a few neurons are wired together, and the wiring itself does something no single cell does. In the knee-jerk reflex a single synapse turns a stretch into a kick, while one extra inhibitory neuron relaxes the muscle that would fight it. In lateral inhibition, each cell in a row holding down its neighbours makes edges stand out: the reason a step in brightness looks like a bright band beside a dark one.

You'll be able to
  • trace the stretch reflex: muscle spindle → Ia fibre → one synapse → α motor neuron → muscle, and add up its latency from distances, speeds and delays
  • explain reciprocal inhibition: the Ia inhibitory interneuron relaxes the opposing muscle, and why strychnine stops it
  • predict what a cut dorsal root, a slow (demyelinated) nerve and an upper motor neuron lesion do to the reflex
  • explain how lateral inhibition exaggerates edges, and why we see Mach bands

Simulation

Start here
  1. Watch the volley go round the loop: the spindle’s Ia fibres carry it to the spinal cord in about 10 ms, the quadriceps motor neuron fires, and about 25 ms after the tap the muscle’s electrical response begins. The kick itself comes later still.
  2. Their motor neuron was firing steadily (orange dots streaming down its axon). The Ia interneuron silences it for about 100 ms, just as the quadriceps fires: reciprocal inhibition.
  3. Strychnine blocks the interneuron’s glycine receptors: the hamstring motor neuron no longer pauses, so both muscles pull at once.
  4. Halve the Ia speed, as in a demyelinating neuropathy: some fibres stop conducting and the rest arrive late and spread out. Only 1 spike in 4 gets through, so the reflex comes late (about 32 ms) and weak.
  5. A light tap, with the motor neuron’s excitability raised as after an upper motor neuron lesion (it has lost the brain’s steadying control). It rests closer to threshold, so the same light tap fires it seven times instead of four.
  6. The volley stops at the cut and never reaches the cord: no reflex at all.
  7. A step in light along a row of receptors. With neighbours inhibiting each other, the firing overshoots on the bright side of the edge and undershoots on the dark side.
  8. A ramp of light between two flat regions, with strong, far-reaching inhibition. Look closely at the bottom strip and the chart: a slightly brighter band at the top of the ramp and a darker one at its foot, though the light has neither.

Theory

In plain words: Tapping the tendon below the kneecap stretches the quadriceps a little. Stretch sensors inside the muscle, the muscle spindles, fire their Ia fibres, the fastest sensory fibres in the body. In the spinal cord each Ia fibre makes an excitatory synapse straight onto the motor neurons of the same muscle: one synapse, so the reflex is called monosynaptic. The motor neurons fire, the quadriceps contracts, and the knee straightens.

The loop

  • Sensor: the muscle spindle, a bundle of small muscle fibres lying alongside the ordinary ones, wrapped by the ending of an Ia fibre. Stretching it opens stretch-gated channels in the ending, and it fires.
  • Into the cord: the Ia fibre’s cell body sits in the dorsal root ganglion; its axon enters the cord through the dorsal root and runs to the ventral horn.
  • One synapse: it releases glutamate onto the α motor neurons of the same muscle.
  • Out again: the motor neurons’ axons leave through the ventral root and run back to the muscle, where each spike crosses the neuromuscular junction and makes the muscle fibres twitch.

How long it takes

Each leg of the trip is a distance divided by a speed, as for a car: time = distance ÷ speed. Add the delays at each stop and you have the reflex’s latency:

\[ t_\text{reflex} = t_\text{spindle} + \frac{L}{\theta_\text{Ia}} + t_\text{cord} + \frac{L}{\theta_\alpha} + t_\text{NMJ} \]

L is the length of the nerve, one way; θIa and θα are the conduction speeds of the Ia fibres and the motor axons (both large and myelinated, as in The Action Potential Travels). A handy trick: 1 m/s is 1 mm per ms. For a leg with 0.6 m between the thigh and the cord:

the tap stretches the spindle, which fires≈ 3 ms
up the Ia fibre: 600 mm ÷ 80 mm/ms7.5 ms
in the cord: the synapse (about 1 ms) and the motor neuron reaching threshold≈ 2 ms
down the motor axon: 600 mm ÷ 60 mm/ms10 ms
the neuromuscular junction, and the muscle’s own spike≈ 2 ms
tap to the muscle’s electrical response≈ 25 ms

Most of the time is travel. The cord adds only a millisecond or two, and that is what told physiologists how many synapses are in the path: Lloyd timed the volley’s arrival in the cord and the motor neurons’ discharge, and the gap was only long enough for one synapse. The kick you see comes later still: a muscle takes tens of milliseconds to build up force. Taller people, with longer nerves, have later reflexes, which is why clinics compare latencies with height.

How one synapse fires the motor neuron

The Ia synapse is Ohm’s law at work. Glutamate opens channels that let Na⁺ in and K⁺ out, with a reversal potential near 0 mV, so the synaptic current is

\[ I_\text{syn} = g_\text{syn}\,(V - E_\text{syn}), \qquad E_\text{syn} \approx 0~\text{mV} \]

At rest, V ≈ −70 mV, so the driving force V − Esyn is −70 mV: a large inward current, which depolarizes the cell. One Ia fibre on its own moves a motor neuron by only a fraction of a millivolt. A tap fires hundreds of Ia fibres together, and their inputs add up on the same membrane (as in Summation and the Spike Trigger Zone). If the sum reaches threshold, the motor neuron fires. The simulation draws the volley of all those fibres as one input, strong enough that a single Ia spike fires the motor neuron; more spikes in the volley make it fire more times, and the muscle pull harder.

Show the derivation

Each step’s time is its distance over its speed. With L in metres and θ in metres per second, L/θ is in seconds; multiply by 1000 for milliseconds. Equivalently, θ in m/s is θ mm per ms, and L in m is 1000·L mm, so

\[ \frac{L}{\theta} = \frac{1000\,L~\text{mm}}{\theta~\text{mm/ms}} = \frac{1000 \times 0.6}{80}~\text{ms} = 7.5~\text{ms} \]

Adding the steps gives the latency. Two of them grow with the nerve’s length, so for a person whose nerve is ΔL longer, the reflex is later by

\[ \Delta t = \Delta L \left( \frac{1}{\theta_\text{Ia}} + \frac{1}{\theta_\alpha} \right) \]

For 0.1 m more, that is 100/80 + 100/60 ≈ 2.9 ms more. In the clinic the same sum is used for the H-reflex: the Ia fibres are stimulated electrically, so tspindle drops out and L is the distance from the stimulating electrode to the cord.

In plain words: The same Ia fibres also excite a small inhibitory neuron in the cord, the Ia inhibitory interneuron. It releases glycine onto the motor neurons of the opposing muscle, the hamstrings, and silences them. So while the quadriceps straightens the knee, the hamstrings, which would bend it, let go. Two synapses: one excites, the next inhibits.

Why glycine holds a neuron down

Glycine opens Cl⁻ channels. By Ohm’s law their current is

\[ I_\text{gly} = g_\text{gly}\,(V - E_\text{Cl}), \qquad E_\text{Cl} \approx -75~\text{mV} \]

Between spikes the hamstring motor neuron climbs from about −70 toward threshold, about −55 mV. At −60 mV the driving force V − ECl is +15 mV: an outward current, which pulls the voltage back toward −75 mV, away from threshold. Even near rest, where the current is small, the open channels add conductance, so excitatory current leaks out through them before it can charge the membrane (shunting, as in Summation and the Spike Trigger Zone).

A worked number. In the simulation the hamstring motor neuron has a leak of gL = 0.1 mS/cm² (EL = −70 mV) and a steady current of 2.6 µA/cm² that keeps it firing. On its own that current would push the voltage to −70 + 2.6 ÷ 0.1 = −44 mV, well past threshold, so the cell fires again and again. Open 0.5 mS/cm² of glycine channels and the voltage settles instead at

\[ V = \frac{g_\text{L} E_\text{L} + g_\text{gly} E_\text{Cl} + I}{g_\text{L} + g_\text{gly}} = \frac{0.1(-70) + 0.5(-75) + 2.6}{0.1 + 0.5} \approx -70~\text{mV} \]

far below threshold: silent until the glycine channels close again, over the next 10 ms or so after each interneuron spike.

Strychnine and tetanus

Strychnine blocks glycine receptors, and tetanus toxin stops inhibitory interneurons releasing glycine (and GABA). Either way, opposing muscles are no longer relaxed when their partners contract. In the simulation, blocking glycine just stops the hamstring motor neuron’s pause. In a body it is worse: as the knee straightens, the stretched hamstrings fire their own stretch reflex, so both muscles pull at once. The result is the spasms and rigidity of strychnine poisoning and of tetanus.

One more synapse, a little later

The inhibition takes one more synapse than the excitation, so it is a little later: in the simulation the interneuron fires about a millisecond after the volley reaches the cord, and the hamstring motor neuron’s next spike is postponed by about 100 ms.

Show the derivation

With a steady injected current I, the voltage stops changing when the currents through the leak and the glycine channels carry it all away:

\[ I = g_\text{L}(V - E_\text{L}) + g_\text{gly}(V - E_\text{Cl}) \]

Collect the terms in V:

\[ V\,(g_\text{L} + g_\text{gly}) = g_\text{L}E_\text{L} + g_\text{gly}E_\text{Cl} + I \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = \frac{g_\text{L}E_\text{L} + g_\text{gly}E_\text{Cl} + I}{g_\text{L} + g_\text{gly}} \]

A conductance-weighted average of the batteries, plus the current divided by the total conductance (I·Rin). The more glycine channels open, the more weight goes on ECl and the smaller I·Rin becomes: both keep V down. (The real cell’s Na⁺ and K⁺ channels change this a little near threshold; the conclusion stands.)

In plain words: Doctors tap tendons because the reflex tests a whole loop: the sensory nerve, the dorsal root, a few segments of spinal cord, the motor neurons, the motor nerve, the neuromuscular junction and the muscle. A missing reflex says the loop is broken somewhere. An exaggerated one says the brain’s control from above has been lost.

An absent or weak reflex

  • A cut or damaged dorsal root, or sensory nerve: the volley never reaches the cord.
  • A demyelinating neuropathy (such as Guillain–Barré syndrome): conduction slows, as in The Action Potential Travels, and some fibres fail to conduct at all (conduction block). Fibres slow by different amounts, so the volley that does arrive is late and spread out in time; its inputs no longer add up together on the motor neuron, and the motor neuron may not reach threshold. Reflexes are often lost early. In the simulation, at 40 m/s the Ia trip doubles from 7.5 to 15 ms and only 1 spike in 4 gets through: the reflex comes at about 32 ms, with a single motor neuron spike.
  • Damage further on: to the motor neurons or their axons, a failing neuromuscular junction (The Neuromuscular Junction), or the muscle itself.

A brisk reflex

Motor neurons are not left to the reflex alone: pathways from the brain hold their excitability in check. After an upper motor neuron lesion (a stroke, a spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis) that control is lost: reflexes become brisk, spread to neighbouring muscles, and may repeat on their own (clonus). Here this is drawn as one steady extra current into the motor neuron, so it rests closer to threshold. By Ohm’s law, a steady current I raises the voltage by about I·Rin: with a leak of 0.1 mS/cm² (Rin = 10 kΩ·cm²), 1.8 µA/cm² raises it 18 mV, to −52 mV. In the simulation the motor neuron rests at about −50 mV instead of −70: close to Ohm’s law, with a little extra from Na⁺ channels starting to open. It does not fire on its own, but a light tap now fires it many times.

Real hyperreflexia has several causes at once (less inhibition from above, less inhibition of the Ia terminals themselves, and motor neurons whose own channels hold them depolarized); the single extra current lumps them together.

Reinforcement works the same way in reverse: asking a patient to clench their hands (the Jendrassik manoeuvre) briefly raises the motor neurons’ excitability, which can bring out a reflex that seemed absent.

In plain words: If every cell in a row inhibits its neighbours, a cell just on the bright side of an edge has dim neighbours on one side, so it is inhibited less than cells deep in the bright region: it fires more. A cell just on the dark side has bright neighbours, so it is inhibited more than cells deep in the dark: it fires less. The edge is exaggerated.

Hartline and Ratliff’s equation

Hartline and Ratliff recorded from single receptors in the horseshoe crab’s eye and found that each one’s firing rate ri is its own excitation ei (the rate it would fire alone, set by its light) minus inhibition from the other receptors’ rates:

\[ r_i = e_i - \sum_{j \ne i} k_{ij}\,r_j \]

kij says how strongly receptor j inhibits receptor i, larger for near neighbours. The inhibition is recurrent: it depends on the neighbours’ output, which is itself inhibited. In the simulation kij falls off with distance, \(k_{ij} \propto e^{-|i-j|/s}\), with s the Reach, and the kij of each receptor add up to K, the Inhibition slider. (Hartline and Ratliff also found each inhibition has a threshold, left out here.)

A worked number

Deep inside an even field every receptor has the same light, so all fire at the same rate r, and the equation becomes r = e − K·r, so

\[ r = \frac{e}{1 + K} \]

With K = 0.6, a bright region (e = 60 spikes/s) fires at 60 ÷ 1.6 = 37.5 spikes/s, and a dark one (e = 20) at 12.5. Both are lower than without inhibition, but by the same factor, so the 3 : 1 ratio is kept. Near the edge the balance changes: the cell on the bright side gets less inhibition (some of its neighbours are dark) and fires more than 37.5; the cell on the dark side gets more and fires less than 12.5. With the defaults the simulation gives 45.5 and 4.5 spikes/s.

Mach bands, and elsewhere

Look at a ramp of brightness between two flat regions and you see a light stripe at the top of the ramp and a dark one at its foot, though the light has neither. Ernst Mach described these bands in 1865; lateral inhibition is the classic explanation. In this simple model the bands are small, a few spikes per second at most (try the Mach bands button): the ones we see look stronger, a sign that the visual system does more than one row of receptors can. The same sharpening happens in the retina, where horizontal cells give retinal cells their centre–surround receptive fields, and in touch, where it helps tell two nearby points apart.

Show the derivation

In an even field ei = e and, far from any edge, rj = r for every j. The sum is then r times the total weight:

\[ r = e - r\sum_{j \ne i} k_{ij} = e - K\,r \quad\Rightarrow\quad r\,(1 + K) = e \quad\Rightarrow\quad r = \frac{e}{1+K} \]

For the edge, a first guess replaces the neighbours’ rates by what they would be in an even field. The cell just on the bright side has about half its inhibition from the dark side (rates near 12.5) and half from the bright side (near 37.5):

\[ r_\text{edge} \approx 60 - 0.6\left(\tfrac12 \cdot 12.5 + \tfrac12 \cdot 37.5\right) = 60 - 15 = 45~\text{spikes/s} \]

more than the 37.5 deep in the bright region. The same guess for the cell just on the dark side gives 20 − 15 = 5 spikes/s, less than 12.5. The simulation solves the equations exactly, by repeating the update until the rates stop changing.
