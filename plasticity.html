Spines and plasticity: where a synapse decides to get stronger or weaker

Most excitatory synapses in the brain end on a dendritic spine: a small head on a thin neck. The spine is the place where a synapse's strength is changed. Its NMDA receptors let Ca²⁺ in only when two things happen at once, its neck keeps that Ca²⁺ in its own head, and how much Ca²⁺ arrives decides whether the synapse is strengthened (LTP) or weakened (LTD). It builds on lesson 5's receptors and lesson 6's dendrites, and introduces a new glutamate receptor, the NMDA receptor, with its Mg²⁺ block.

You'll be able to
  • explain why NMDA receptors are coincidence detectors: Ca²⁺ flows only with glutamate bound and Post (the postsynaptic cell, as in lesson 5) depolarized enough to push Mg²⁺ out of the pore
  • predict LTP or LTD from the stimulation: a lot of Ca²⁺ fast (a 100 Hz tetanus, or pairing) gives LTP; a little Ca²⁺ for a long time (1 Hz for 15 min) gives LTD
  • explain that both are expressed as more or fewer AMPA receptors, and that blocking NMDA receptors (APV) prevents both without changing the ordinary EPSP
  • explain input specificity: only the stimulated spine changes, because its Ca²⁺ stays in its head
  • explain why an EPSP is biggest in the spine head and smaller and slower at the soma (lesson 2's RC filter, lesson 3's cable)

  • Spine B gets almost no Ca²⁺ even though its voltage did the same thing. Whatever the Ca²⁺ does next happens only in spine A: input specificity.
  • Untick Mg²⁺: glutamate alone now lets in a lot of Ca²⁺, which is why low-Mg²⁺ solutions make slices prone to plasticity (and seizures).

  • Time is compressed: the hour plays in a few seconds. Each run starts from where the last one left the synapse; Reset puts it back to the start.
  • Tetanus: 100 pulses 10 ms apart. Each EPSP lands before the last has gone (temporal summation), Post depolarizes, the Mg²⁺ comes out, and Ca²⁺ climbs well above the CaMKII level. AMPA receptors are added and the EPSP stays bigger: LTP. The first few minutes are bigger still: post-tetanic potentiation, a short-lived presynaptic effect.
  • 1 Hz: each pulse gives only a little Ca²⁺, which never reaches the CaMKII level. But 900 of them keep calcineurin switched on, and AMPA receptors are removed: LTD.
  • Pairing: only 1 Hz, but each pulse arrives while Post is depolarized, so each lets in a lot of Ca²⁺: LTP from a slow rate. What matters is the Ca²⁺, not the frequency itself.
  • Spine B never changes. With APV no protocol does anything, yet the test EPSP is just as big: it runs through AMPA receptors, and APV blocks only the NMDA receptors that start the change.
  • Run LTP, then 1 Hz: the potentiated synapse is brought back down (depotentiation).

Theory

In plain words: The synaptic current enters a tiny head and has to squeeze through the neck to reach the dendrite. By Ohm's law the head sits above the dendrite by the current times the neck's resistance, so the EPSP is biggest there. On its way to the soma it charges bigger and bigger capacitors through resistors, so it arrives smaller and slower.

Ohm's law across the neck

While the synaptic current Isyn flows out of the head through the neck, the voltage difference across the neck is

\[ V_\mathrm{head} - V_\mathrm{dend} = I_\mathrm{syn}\,R_\mathrm{neck} \]

A few tens of pA through a few hundred MΩ is several mV, so the head can depolarize much more than the dendrite. That is useful for Part 2: a bigger depolarization in the head pushes more Mg²⁺ out of its own NMDA receptors.

A low-pass filter

Each piece of membrane is lesson 2's RC circuit. The head is tiny (small C), so it charges in a fraction of a millisecond. The dendrite and soma are big capacitors, charged through resistors, so they rise and fall more slowly: each step is a low-pass filter with its own τ = RC. A fast, tall EPSP in the head becomes a slow, small one at the soma.

How much does the neck matter?

The size of the neck's resistance is still argued about: estimates range from tens to hundreds of MΩ, and necks change shape with activity. What is not in doubt is that the neck holds Ca²⁺ in the head (Part 2). Most of the attenuation on the way to the soma happens along the dendrite, the subject of lessons 3 and 6.

Show the equations

Each compartment obeys lesson 2's equation with an extra current for each neighbour it is joined to. For the head:

\[ C_\mathrm{head}\frac{dV_\mathrm{head}}{dt} = -g_\mathrm{L}(V_\mathrm{head} - E_\mathrm{L}) - \frac{V_\mathrm{head} - V_\mathrm{dend}}{R_\mathrm{neck}} - I_\mathrm{syn} \]

The dendrite has the same terms for the leak, for each neck (with the sign turned round) and for the resistance to the soma. Here Chead = 0.5 pF, the dendrite 20 pF and the soma 60 pF, each with τ = 20 ms at rest, and 500 MΩ between the dendrite and the soma.

In plain words: An NMDA receptor is a channel that glutamate opens, but at rest a Mg²⁺ ion sits in its pore and blocks it. Depolarization pushes the Mg²⁺ out. So Ca²⁺ flows only when Pre released glutamate and Post is depolarized at the same time.

A channel with a voltage-dependent plug

Like any synaptic current in lesson 5, the NMDA current is a conductance times a driving force, multiplied by the fraction of receptors not plugged by Mg²⁺:

\[ I_\mathrm{NMDA} = g_\mathrm{NMDA}\cdot f_\mathrm{free}(V)\cdot(V - E_\mathrm{NMDA}), \qquad f_\mathrm{free}(V) = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{[\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}]}{3.57\ \mathrm{mM}}\,e^{-0.062\,V}} \]

With 1 mM Mg²⁺, about 4 % are free at −70 mV, 14 % at −50 mV and 65 % at −10 mV. So the same glutamate lets through many times more current, and Ca²⁺, when Post is depolarized.

Where the depolarization comes from

  • Many inputs at once, or fast: summation (lesson 6) depolarizes the dendrite; a tetanus does this by itself.
  • A spike that runs back from the axon into the dendrites (a back-propagating action potential), arriving just after the glutamate.
  • An electrode holding Post depolarized: the pairing protocol of experiments.

Why the Ca²⁺ stays in its spine

The neck is narrow, and the head has its own pumps and Ca²⁺-binding proteins, so Ca²⁺ is removed before much can spread to the dendrite or its neighbours. Each spine is its own small Ca²⁺ compartment, so each synapse's strength can be changed on its own: input specificity.

In plain words: Ca²⁺ in the spine switches on two enzymes. A lot of Ca²⁺, fast, switches on CaMKII, which adds AMPA receptors: LTP. A little Ca²⁺ for a long time switches on calcineurin, which takes AMPA receptors away: LTD. The same ion gives opposite results depending on how much arrives and for how long.

Two enzymes, two levels

  • CaMKII (Ca²⁺/calmodulin-dependent kinase II) needs a high Ca²⁺ level. Once on, it adds phosphate groups to AMPA receptors and to the proteins that hold them, and brings more AMPA receptors into the synapse. Each glutamate release now opens more channels: a bigger EPSP.
  • Calcineurin is a phosphatase (it removes phosphate groups). It responds to much less Ca²⁺ but slowly, so a modest rise has to be kept up for minutes. It leads to AMPA receptors being pulled back into the cell: a smaller EPSP.

When both are on, as during a tetanus, CaMKII wins. The test pulses every 30 s give too little Ca²⁺, too rarely, to switch on either.

Why the frequency matters

At 100 Hz each EPSP arrives before the last has gone, so Post depolarizes, Mg²⁺ comes out, and every pulse lets in more Ca²⁺ than the one before. At 1 Hz each EPSP is over long before the next, so Post stays near rest and each pulse lets in only a little. Pairing breaks the link: a 1 Hz pulse with Post held depolarized lets in as much as a tetanus does.

Early and late LTP

In the first hour, LTP is mostly receptors moved into the synapse and existing proteins modified, as modelled here. Lasting longer needs new proteins made in the cell, and the spine head grows to hold the extra receptors (drawn here as a bigger head A after LTP). Without that, early LTP fades within a few hours.

Show the rule used here

The protocols are run at 1 ms steps. Each pulse adds Ca²⁺ in proportion to the fraction of NMDA receptors free of Mg²⁺ at that moment's voltage, and Ca²⁺ is removed with τ = 30 ms. CaMKII collects the time Ca²⁺ spends above its level (3 µM); calcineurin follows Ca²⁺ / (Ca²⁺ + 0.5 µM) with τ = 10 s, and LTD collects the time it spends above 0.1:

\[ \Delta w = 0.25\!\int\!\big([\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}] - 3\big)_+\,dt \;-\; 0.0015\!\int\!\big(\text{calcineurin} - 0.1\big)_+\,dt \]

w is the number of AMPA receptors as a fraction of the start (10 receptors), kept between 40 % and 220 %. This is a simplified version of the Ca²⁺-level rule of Lisman (1989) and Shouval, Bear and Cooper (2002).

Ca²⁺ above the CaMKII level for most of a second: LTP.
