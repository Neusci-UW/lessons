The neuromuscular junction: the synapse built not to fail

Where a motor nerve meets a skeletal muscle fibre is a chemical synapse like the ones in the Chemical Synapse lesson, but built for certainty. A synapse in the brain makes an EPSP of well under a millivolt, and a neuron needs many at once. Here one nerve impulse releases dozens of packets of acetylcholine (ACh), about three times as many as it takes to fire the muscle fibre, and makes an end-plate potential (EPP) of some 40 mV where 20 would do. That margin is the safety factor, and it is what myasthenia gravis, Lambert–Eaton syndrome and the muscle relaxants used in surgery all eat into.

You'll be able to
  • follow one nerve impulse to one muscle impulse: Ca²⁺ entry, ACh released in quanta, nicotinic receptors, the end-plate potential, the muscle's Na⁺ channels, the muscle action potential
  • define a quantum, a miniature EPP, the quantal content (m = n·p) and the safety factor, and work a safety factor out from numbers
  • calculate the end-plate current with Ohm's law, IEPC = gACh(V − EACh), and explain why a big EPP is smaller than the sum of its quanta
  • predict what fewer receptors, a lower release probability, fewer working release sites or a blocked acetylcholinesterase do to the EPP, and use the miniature EPPs to tell a problem before the cleft from one after it
  • explain the clinical tests: why the response shrinks during a 3 Hz train in myasthenia gravis (a decrement) and grows at 50 Hz in Lambert–Eaton syndrome (an increment)

Simulation

Start here
  1. Five impulses at 3 Hz, as in the clinic's repetitive-stimulation test. Each releases fewer quanta than the one before, as the docked vesicles run down, but every EPP stays far above threshold and every fibre in the muscle keeps firing.
  2. Antibodies have removed more than half the ACh receptors, so each quantum does less than half its usual work. The first EPPs still make it; by the fourth the run-down of release leaves the EPP short and the fibre fails. Across the whole muscle, fibres drop out one by one: the decrement.
  3. Block most of the acetylcholinesterase in that myasthenic junction. ACh lingers in the cleft, each quantum acts longer, and the EPPs clear threshold again: this is how pyridostigmine treats myasthenia.
  4. Antibodies against the terminal's Ca²⁺ channels drop the release probability from 0.2 to 0.05, and the EPP falls short. At 50 Hz the Ca²⁺ left over from each impulse builds up, release grows, and the fibre starts firing: the increment.
  5. The toxin stops most release sites working. Vesicles stay docked but cannot fuse: few quanta per impulse, and far fewer miniature EPPs, each of its normal size.
  6. Curare sits on most of the receptors. One impulse now leaves a small EPP that never reaches threshold: this is how Fatt and Katz first studied the EPP on its own.

Theory

In plain words: This is the Chemical Synapse lesson's synapse, built big. One nerve impulse releases dozens of vesicles of acetylcholine at once; ACh opens receptor channels on the muscle; Na⁺ rushes in; the end-plate depolarizes well past threshold; and the muscle fibre's own Na⁺ channels fire an action potential that runs along the fibre and makes it contract.

Six steps in two milliseconds

  1. The nerve impulse reaches the terminal and depolarizes it.
  2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (the P/Q type) at the active zones open, and Ca²⁺ rushes in.
  3. Ca²⁺ makes docked vesicles fuse with the membrane and empty their ACh into the cleft, about half a millisecond after the impulse arrives.
  4. ACh crosses the 50 nm cleft and opens nicotinic receptors, channels for Na⁺ and K⁺ packed on the crests of the folds. The current through them depolarizes the end-plate: the end-plate potential.
  5. Acetylcholinesterase in the cleft splits ACh within about a millisecond, so the receptors close again.
  6. If the EPP reached threshold, Na⁺ channels deep in the folds fire a muscle action potential, which spreads along the fibre and triggers contraction.

The end-plate current: Ohm's law with its own battery

An open nicotinic receptor is a conductance like any other, and the current through it is that conductance times the driving force, the distance from the voltage to the channel's own battery:

\[ I_\mathrm{EPC} = g_\mathrm{ACh}(t)\,\bigl(V - E_\mathrm{ACh}\bigr), \qquad E_\mathrm{ACh} \approx -5\ \mathrm{mV} \]

The channel passes both Na⁺ and K⁺, so its battery sits between ENa (about +60 mV) and EK (about −95 mV), a little below 0 mV. Takeuchi and Takeuchi measured it by holding the end-plate at different voltages and finding where the current reversed. At rest the driving force is large: V − EACh = −85 − (−5) = −80 mV. If the open receptors come to 4 µS at the peak, the current is 4 µS × (−80 mV) = −320 nA, flowing into the fibre. (Negative means inward, the way electrophysiologists count it.)

Show where EACh ≈ −5 mV comes from

Inside the channel, Na⁺ and K⁺ are two conductances side by side, each pulling toward its own battery. The channel passes no net current where the two cancel:

\[ g_\mathrm{Na}\,(E_\mathrm{ACh} - E_\mathrm{Na}) + g_\mathrm{K}\,(E_\mathrm{ACh} - E_\mathrm{K}) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad E_\mathrm{ACh} = \frac{g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K}}{g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}} \]

which is the conductance-weighted average, the same chord-conductance equation as in the Membrane Potential lesson. With ENa = +60 mV, EK = −95 mV and gNa about 1.4 times gK: EACh = (1.4 × 60 − 95) / 2.4 ≈ −5 mV.

From current to voltage

The end-plate current joins the circuit from the Passive Properties: Time lesson: it charges the membrane's capacitor, while the resting conductance leaks it away.

\[ C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = -\,g_\mathrm{rest}\,(V - E_\mathrm{rest}) \;-\; g_\mathrm{ACh}(t)\,(V - E_\mathrm{ACh}) \]

This is the equation the simulation solves, step by step, for the end-plate. Because gACh here is huge next to a brain synapse's, V moves a long way toward EACh: about 45 mV in a healthy junction, when the fibre needs only 20.

Why this synapse is not like one in the brain

A neuron in the brain collects thousands of small synapses and adds them up (the Summation lesson). A muscle fibre has one junction, and that junction alone has to fire it every single time. So it is one very large synapse: hundreds of active zones, dozens of vesicles per impulse, receptors packed at about 10 000 per square micrometre on the fold crests, and the folds themselves, which put a dense patch of the muscle's Na⁺ channels right under those receptors.

In plain words: Even at rest a vesicle fuses now and then by itself and makes a blip of about a millivolt, a miniature EPP. The EPP is just many of those at once: a whole number of packets, the quanta. How big the packets are, and how many come out, tells you where a sick junction's problem is.

Miniature EPPs

Fatt and Katz, recording from inside a frog muscle fibre at its end-plate, saw small depolarizations arriving at random, about one a second, each a fraction of a millivolt, with the same shape as the EPP. Curare made them smaller and neostigmine made them bigger and longer, so they came from ACh acting on the receptors. Each is the ACh of one vesicle, a few thousand molecules: one quantum, of size q.

The EPP is built from quanta

Del Castillo and Katz lowered the Ca²⁺ outside until an impulse released only a few quanta. The EPPs then came in steps the size of one mEPP: 0, q, 2q, 3q… An impulse releases a whole number of quanta, the quantal content, m. If the terminal has n release sites and each releases with probability p,

\[ m = n\,p, \qquad \text{EPP} \approx m \times q \quad (\text{while the EPP is small}) \]

In the simulation n = 300 and p = 0.2, so m = 300 × 0.2 = 60 quanta per impulse, and one quantum makes a miniature EPP of about 1.2 mV. Rat end-plates release about 50 to 80 quanta per impulse (Wood and Slater), a frog's a few hundred.

Each disorder changes a different number, and the miniature EPPs show which:

A small q means fewer working receptors, so the trouble is after the cleft. A small m with a normal q means less is being released, so it is before the cleft. Elmqvist and colleagues found exactly this pattern in muscle from people with myasthenia gravis: miniature EPPs about a fifth of normal size, with a normal number of quanta per impulse.

Show how much m varies from impulse to impulse

Each of the n sites releases or not by chance, so m follows the binomial distribution (the Chemical Synapse lesson), with mean n·p and standard deviation

\[ \sigma_m = \sqrt{n\,p\,(1-p)} = \sqrt{300 \times 0.2 \times 0.8} \approx 6.9 \]

So an impulse releases 60 ± 7 quanta, about ±12%: small next to a threefold margin, which is why the simulation just uses the average. At a brain synapse with n = 10 and p = 0.3, the same sum gives 3 ± 1.4 quanta, about ±50%, and some impulses release nothing at all.

In plain words: The nerve releases about three times as many quanta as it takes to reach threshold. That margin, the safety factor, is why your muscles don't skip a beat as release runs down during use, and it is what every disease of the junction eats into. Above 1 the fibre fires; below 1 it fails.

The safety factor

Wood and Slater define it as the quanta an impulse releases divided by the quanta it takes to reach threshold:

\[ \text{safety factor} = \frac{m}{m_\mathrm{needed}} \]

In the simulation the fibre rests at −85 mV and fires at about −65 mV, so the EPP has to lift it 20 mV. That takes about 20 quanta, and a healthy impulse releases 60: a safety factor of 60 / 20 = 3. Wood and Slater, counting quanta from voltage recordings in the rat's soleus muscle, found 62 released and 20 needed, the same 3; counting them more carefully from the current, they put it at 3.5 in the soleus and 5.0 in a faster muscle. Between 3 and 5 is usual in adult mammals.

Why a big EPP is less than the sum of its quanta

Each quantum adds conductance, not voltage. The first quanta push V up from −85 mV, where the driving force is a full 80 mV. As V climbs toward EACh that driving force shrinks, so each extra quantum adds less than the one before: each closes about the same fraction of the gap that is left. For a current much briefer than the membrane's time constant this gives

\[ \text{EPP} \approx D\,\bigl(1 - e^{-m q / D}\bigr), \qquad D = E_\mathrm{ACh} - V_\mathrm{rest} \]

With D = 80 mV, q = 1 mV and m = 60: EPP ≈ 80 × (1 − e−0.75) ≈ 42 mV, not 60. The EPP can approach EACh but never pass it. The calculator below works this out for any numbers you like.

Show the derivation

While a brief end-plate current flows, the resting conductance has little time to act, so for those moments

\[ C\,\frac{dV}{dt} \approx -\,g_\mathrm{ACh}(t)\,(V - E_\mathrm{ACh}) \quad\Rightarrow\quad V - E_\mathrm{ACh} = (V_\mathrm{rest} - E_\mathrm{ACh})\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{C}\textstyle\int g_\mathrm{ACh}\,dt\Bigr) \]

The distance left to EACh shrinks by the same factor for each bit of conductance. One quantum on its own, starting from rest, moves V by q ≈ D × (∫g₁ dt / C), so ∫g₁ dt / C ≈ q / D. Then m quanta give m times that, and

\[ \text{EPP} = D - D\,e^{-m q/D} = D\,\bigl(1 - e^{-m q / D}\bigr) \]

For small m q this is just m × q. Turning it around gives the quanta needed to lift V by N millivolts: mneeded = (D / q) ln(D / (D − N)). Martin's classic correction, EPP = m q / (1 + m q / D), comes from the same idea for a conductance left on until V settles, and gives a somewhat smaller EPP. The simulation follows V moment by moment and lands between the two.

The folds add to the margin

The junctional folds put a dense patch of the muscle's Na⁺ channels right under the receptors, so the end-plate fires at a lower threshold than the rest of the fibre. Wood and Slater found that this alone doubles the safety factor. Frogs get their margin by releasing a great deal of transmitter; mammals release less and lean on the folds.

Is a quarter of the healthy release enough?

In plain words: Each impulse uses up some of the docked vesicles, and they take a second or two to come back, so later impulses release fewer quanta (depression). Each impulse also leaves a little Ca²⁺ behind, which makes the next one release more (facilitation). At a healthy junction both happen, and the safety factor covers the dip.

Depression: the docked vesicles run down

Let R be the share of docking sites with a vesicle ready. An impulse releases m = n p R quanta and leaves R smaller by the fraction p; between impulses the empty sites refill, with a time constant of about 1.5 s. At 3 Hz the next impulse comes long before refilling is done, so in the simulation the quanta fall 60 → 52 → 48 → 45 → 44 and then level off, where refilling just keeps up with use. The fifth impulse releases about a quarter less than the first, and a healthy junction, with three times what it needs, never notices.

Facilitation: the Ca²⁺ left over

The Ca²⁺ that enters with an impulse takes tens of milliseconds to be pumped out of the terminal. A second impulse soon after adds its own Ca²⁺ on top, and because release goes as about the fourth power of Ca²⁺ (Dodge and Rahamimoff; the Chemical Synapse lesson), a little extra Ca²⁺ raises release a lot. At 3 Hz the leftover Ca²⁺ is long gone before the next impulse. At 50 Hz it piles up: 46% extra Ca²⁺ gives 1.46⁴ ≈ 4.5 times the release probability.

Which one wins depends on p

A junction with a high p (healthy, p = 0.2) uses up its docked vesicles quickly, so at 50 Hz it facilitates for an impulse or two and then depresses. A junction with a low p (Lambert–Eaton, p = 0.05) uses few vesicles per impulse, so the extra Ca²⁺ wins and release keeps climbing. That difference is what the clinical tests in the next tab are built on.

Show the simulation's equations

Before impulse k, with Rk the share of sites docked and ck the leftover Ca²⁺ (as a fraction of what one impulse brings to the release sites):

\[ p_k = 1 - (1 - p)^{(1 + c_k)^4}, \qquad m_k = n\,p_k\,R_k, \qquad R \to R\,(1 - p_k), \qquad c \to c + 0.18 \]

The odd-looking form of pk behaves like p (1 + ck)⁴ while that is small but can never pass 1. Between impulses c decays with a time constant of 60 ms, and empty sites refill with a time constant of 1.5 s, faster while c is high, which is how a terminal keeps up during a fast train.

In plain words: Each disorder breaks one link in the chain. Problems after the cleft (myasthenia gravis, curare) make each quantum do less; problems before it (Lambert–Eaton, botulinum toxin) mean fewer quanta. Either way the safety factor shrinks, and once it drops below 1 the fibre stops firing.

Myasthenia gravis

Antibodies against the ACh receptor make the muscle lose receptors and flatten the fold crests; Fambrough and colleagues counted the receptors at patients' end-plates and found far fewer. Each quantum then opens fewer channels, so the miniature EPPs are small. A rested muscle may still work, but with the margin near 1 the normal run-down during use pushes EPPs below threshold: muscles weaken as they are used, often the eyelids and eye muscles first. In patients the receptor loss is usually greater than in this simulation, and the nerve makes up part of it by releasing more quanta per impulse (Plomp and colleagues). The treatments are acetylcholinesterase blockers such as pyridostigmine, and drugs that calm the immune attack.

Lambert–Eaton syndrome

Antibodies against the terminal's P/Q-type Ca²⁺ channels; Lang and colleagues showed that patients' antibodies alone can cause it. Less Ca²⁺ enters, and because release goes as about the fourth power of Ca²⁺ entry, losing 30% of the Ca²⁺ entry cuts release to about a quarter: 0.7⁴ ≈ 0.24. That is the simulation's Lambert–Eaton setting, a release probability of 0.05 instead of 0.2; in the picture about 3 in 10 Ca²⁺ channels are grey. Few quanta leave the terminal, so the EPP falls short. Fast stimulation, or ten seconds of hard voluntary contraction, lets Ca²⁺ build up and release grows. About half the people with it have small-cell lung cancer, whose cells carry the same kind of Ca²⁺ channel.

Botulinum toxin

The toxin is an enzyme that gets into the terminal and cuts a SNARE protein that vesicles need in order to fuse (Schiavo and colleagues). Vesicles still dock but cannot empty, so release sites stop working: fewer quanta per impulse, far fewer miniature EPPs, each of normal size. Swallowed in spoiled food it causes botulism, a weakness that spreads from the face and throat. Injected locally in tiny doses it is a treatment, quieting overactive muscles for months, until the terminal sprouts and builds new release sites.

Curare and the muscle relaxants used in surgery

Curare (d-tubocurarine) sits in the ACh binding site without opening the channel, competing with ACh. Rocuronium and vecuronium work the same way and are used to relax muscles during surgery. With part of the receptors blocked the EPP is small and fades during a train, much as in myasthenia gravis. Neostigmine reverses the block: with acetylcholinesterase stopped, ACh lingers and outcompetes the blocker.

Acetylcholinesterase blockers

Neostigmine and pyridostigmine block the enzyme for hours, so the ACh of each quantum stays around longer and opens more receptors, and the EPP grows. That is a treatment for myasthenia gravis and the way anaesthetists reverse a muscle relaxant. Too much, though, and the end-plate never repolarizes: its Na⁺ channels inactivate, as in the Hodgkin–Huxley lesson, and the fibre stops responding again.

In plain words: A clinician cannot record from one end-plate, but can stimulate a nerve through the skin and record the whole muscle's response, the compound muscle action potential: the muscle's version of the nerve's compound action potential in the Recording from Outside lesson. Its size counts how many fibres fired. One fibre is all-or-none; the muscle is not

Each fibre either fires or fails, but the fibres of one muscle are not identical: some end-plates have more receptors or release more quanta than others. In the simulation each fibre's quanta × quantal size varies around the typical fibre's by about 30%. When the junctions are weak, the fibres with the least margin fail first, so the whole muscle's response falls away one fibre at a time.

Repetitive nerve stimulation

  • Myasthenia gravis: stimulate at 2 to 3 Hz and compare the 4th or 5th response with the 1st. A fall of more than about 10% is a decrement: the run-down of release pushes more and more fibres below threshold. It is deepest at the 4th or 5th impulse and then levels off, as refilling catches up.
  • Lambert–Eaton: the first response is small, because many fibres fail even at rest. Stimulate at 20 to 50 Hz, or record again right after ten seconds of maximal contraction, and it grows; a rise of 100% or more is the classic sign. Botulism can show a smaller increment for the same reason.

Train-of-four in the operating room

Anaesthetists check how much muscle relaxant is still acting by stimulating a nerve four times at 2 Hz and comparing the 4th twitch with the 1st. With part of the receptors blocked the response fades, as in myasthenia gravis, and the patient is not ready to breathe on their own until the 4th twitch is back to at least 90% of the 1st.
