Charging a membrane: the RC circuit and the ions

When ion channels open, why doesn't the membrane potential jump straight to its new value? Watch a membrane charge like a capacitor, first as an electric circuit and then ion by ion, and explain it with the theory below.

You'll be able to
  • explain why current first goes into charging the membrane, the capacitor, before it can flow through the other channels (IC = C dV/dt)
  • predict where the voltage ends up (V∞) and how fast it gets there (τ = C / Σg)
  • explain why at steady state Na⁺ flowing in matches K⁺ flowing out, so the charge on the membrane stops changing even though ions keep crossing

  • Close the switch (or open the Na⁺ channels). At first almost all the Na⁺ current goes into the capacitor: the plates fill, and in the particle model Na⁺ ions pile up as positive charge just inside the membrane.
  • As the voltage rises, the K⁺ branch carries more and more current outward and the Na⁺ branch less inward, because both are now further from (or closer to) their batteries.
  • Once the voltage stops changing, the capacitor current is zero. Current now goes all the way around: in through Na⁺, out through K⁺. The charge on the membrane is constant, yet ions keep crossing.
  • Open the switch again and the capacitor discharges through the K⁺ branch alone, more slowly, because there is less conductance.

Units

The circuit: real units

mV
millivolts, for voltage. V is the inside measured relative to the outside.
ms
milliseconds, for time. The charts show the last 40 ms; the speed slider sets how many ms of model time pass per second.
µA/cm²
microamps per cm² of membrane, for current, positive when it flows outward.
mS/cm²
millisiemens per cm², for conductance, how easily current flows (1/resistance). One siemens passes one amp per volt, from Ohm's law.
µF/cm²
microfarads per cm², for capacitance, the charge stored per volt. Real membranes are about 1 µF/cm².
nC/cm²
nanocoulombs per cm², for charge: Q (nC/cm²) = C (µF/cm²) × V (mV).

These fit together: µA/cm² divided by µF/cm² is mV/ms, and µF/cm² divided by mS/cm² is ms, so τ = C/Σg comes out in milliseconds.

The particle model: simulation units

ions
charge counted in ions: each K⁺ or Na⁺ carries one elementary charge, 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. "Charge on the membrane" and the Ions moved chart count this way.
steps
the simulation's unit of time: in each step every ion takes one random move. Steps are not tied to real milliseconds, so use the circuit for real times. Currents are in ions per 1000 steps.
e/mV
the particle model's membrane capacitance: extra ions needed on the membrane to change the voltage by 1 mV. The same idea as µF/cm², in simulation units.
channels
conductance in the particle model is a count of open channels in the simulated patch: more channels, more current for the same driving force.

Theory

Ohm's law for each channel

Ohm's law says the current through a resistor is its conductance times the voltage across it, \(I = gV\) (with \(g = 1/R\)). An ion channel is a resistor in series with a battery, the ion's Nernst potential \(E\), so the voltage that drives current through it is the driving force \(V - E\):

\[ I_\mathrm{Na} = g_\mathrm{Na}\,(V - E_\mathrm{Na}), \qquad I_\mathrm{K} = g_\mathrm{K}\,(V - E_\mathrm{K}) \]

Each current is zero at its own Nernst potential and grows the farther V is from it; positive means outward.

The capacitor

The voltage across a capacitor is the charge on it divided by its capacitance. So the only way to change the voltage is to move charge onto or off the plates, and the current into the capacitor is how fast that charge moves:

\[ Q = C\,V \quad\Rightarrow\quad I_C = \frac{dQ}{dt} = C\,\frac{dV}{dt} \]

Charge cannot pile up on the wires, so whatever the channels carry across must come off the capacitor: the currents at a junction add up to zero (Kirchhoff's current law). The voltage therefore changes at a rate set by the net channel current:

\[ I_C + I_\mathrm{Na} + I_\mathrm{K} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{dV}{dt} = -\,\frac{I_\mathrm{Na} + I_\mathrm{K}}{C} \]

A quick number

With currents in µA/cm² and C in µF/cm², the rate comes out in mV/ms: 100 µA/cm² into 1 µF/cm² moves V at 100 mV/ms. At the default settings, opening the Na⁺ channels at rest gives \(I_\mathrm{Na} = 0.7 \times (-86 - 60) \approx -102\) µA/cm² while \(I_\mathrm{K} = 0\), so V starts climbing at about 100 mV/ms.

Why it slows down

As V moves toward the voltage where the channel currents cancel, \(V_\infty\), those currents shrink, so the capacitor charges more and more slowly. The rate turns out to be proportional to the distance V still has to go:

\[ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{V_\infty - V}{\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{C}{g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}} \]

A rate proportional to the distance still to go gives an exponential approach:

\[ V(t) = V_\infty + \left(V_0 - V_\infty\right) e^{-t/\tau} \]

At the defaults \(\tau = 1/(0.7 + 0.5) \approx 0.83\) ms: V covers about 63% of the way in one τ and 95% in three. More capacitance means more charge to move, so τ is longer; more conductance means more current to move it with, so τ is shorter. Neither changes where V ends up.

Show the derivation

1. Put the Ohm's law currents into the rate equation:

\[ \frac{dV}{dt} = -\,\frac{g_\mathrm{Na}(V - E_\mathrm{Na}) + g_\mathrm{K}(V - E_\mathrm{K})}{C} \]

2. Collect the terms with V:

\[ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}}{C}\left(\frac{g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K}}{g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}} - V\right) = \frac{V_\infty - V}{\tau} \]

3. The exponential solves this: differentiating \(V_\infty + (V_0 - V_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}\) gives \(-(V_0 - V_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}/\tau = (V_\infty - V)/\tau\), and at \(t = 0\) it starts at \(V_0\).

Try it

Twice the capacitance: τ doubles, and V takes twice as long to settle at the same V∞.

Half the capacitance: V settles twice as fast.

Steady state

V stops changing when no current is charging the capacitor, so the channel currents cancel: Na⁺ current in is matched by K⁺ current out. That happens at

\[ V_\infty = \frac{g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K}}{g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}} \]

This is the chord conductance formula from the Membrane potential page: an average of the Nernst potentials, each weighted by its share of the conductance. Opening Na⁺ channels pulls \(V_\infty\) toward \(E_\mathrm{Na}\); more K⁺ conductance pulls it back toward \(E_\mathrm{K}\).

Show the derivation

Set \(dV/dt = 0\), so the channel currents add to zero, and solve for V:

\[ g_\mathrm{Na}(V_\infty - E_\mathrm{Na}) + g_\mathrm{K}(V_\infty - E_\mathrm{K}) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_\infty\,(g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}) = g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K} \]

And GHK

Real channels, and the ones in the particle model, are not perfect resistors: their currents follow the GHK current equation, so the voltage where they cancel is the GHK voltage:

\[ V_\infty = \frac{RT}{F}\,\ln\frac{P_\mathrm{K}[\mathrm{K}^+]_o + P_\mathrm{Na}[\mathrm{Na}^+]_o}{P_\mathrm{K}[\mathrm{K}^+]_i + P_\mathrm{Na}[\mathrm{Na}^+]_i} \]

The circuit uses fixed g, so its \(V_\infty\) is close to, but not exactly, the GHK value; in the particle model the voltage settles on the GHK line.

With the Na⁺ channels open, V∞ moves much closer to ENa.

In the particle model

The same rule is a count. Each net ion that crosses adds one charge to the membrane, which moves V by a fixed step: about 2 mV at the default Membrane C (the bilayer's capacitance in series with the charge layers in the solution). On the Ions moved chart, "on membrane" is that net count, and the voltage trace follows its shape.

Where these ideas come from

The original papers behind this lesson, oldest first within each idea. Most are free to read through the DOI links.

  1. Lapicque L (1907). Recherches quantitatives sur l'excitation électrique des nerfs traitée comme une polarisation. Journal de Physiologie et de Pathologie Générale 9:620–635.
    The membrane as a capacitor charged by a stimulus current through a leak: the first RC model of a nerve.
  2. Fricke H (1925). The electric capacity of suspensions with special reference to blood. Journal of General Physiology 9:137–152. doi:10.1085/jgp.9.2.137
    The first measurement of a cell membrane's capacitance, about 1 µF/cm², and from it the membrane's thinness.
  3. Cole KS, Curtis HJ (1938). Transverse electric impedance of the squid giant axon. Journal of General Physiology 21:757–765. doi:10.1085/jgp.21.6.757
    The squid axon membrane as a capacitor of about 1 µF/cm² in parallel with a resistance.
  4. Cole KS, Curtis HJ (1939). Electric impedance of the squid giant axon during activity. Journal of General Physiology 22:649–670. doi:10.1085/jgp.22.5.649
    During a spike the membrane's conductance rises about forty-fold while its capacitance stays the same.
  5. Hodgkin AL, Rushton WAH (1946). The electrical constants of a crustacean nerve fibre. Proceedings of the Royal Society of London B 133:444–479. doi:10.1098/rspb.1946.0024
    Cable theory for an axon: membrane resistance and capacitance, the time constant τ and the length constant λ.
  6. Nernst W (1889). Die elektromotorische Wirksamkeit der Ionen. Zeitschrift für physikalische Chemie 4:129–181.
    The Nernst equation: the voltage at which an ion's concentration difference is balanced.
  7. Hodgkin AL, Huxley AF (1952). A quantitative description of membrane current and its application to conduction and excitation in nerve. Journal of Physiology 117:500–544. doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1952.sp004764
    The membrane as Na⁺ and K⁺ conductances with Nernst batteries and a capacitor, the gates m, h and n, and a computed travelling spike. The channels in lessons 3 to 5 are theirs, including the 3× speed-up per 10 °C.
