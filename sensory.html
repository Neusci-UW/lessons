Sensory receptors: from stimulus to spikes

In most of the other lessons the first current comes from an electrode. In your body it comes from the world: a touch, a stretch, light, a chemical. A sensory ending turns the stimulus into a receptor potential, a graded depolarization like an EPSP, and the first node of Ranvier on its axon turns that into a train of spikes whose rate tells the brain how strong the stimulus is. Endings also adapt differently, so the same press is reported as “something is touching you” by one kind and as “something just changed” by another.

You'll be able to
  • trace the chain from stimulus to spikes: force opens mechanically gated channels (Piezo2) → receptor current → receptor potential → spikes at the first node
  • calculate a receptor potential with Ohm’s law, from the fraction of channels open and their battery near 0 mV
  • explain why the receptor potential is graded and the spikes are not, and how firing rate carries the stimulus strength
  • explain why the response is compressed, growing roughly with the logarithm of the stimulus
  • contrast slowly adapting endings (Merkel: steady pressure) with rapidly adapting ones (Pacinian: change and vibration), and say what the Pacinian capsule does

Simulation

Start here
  1. A Merkel ending under a probe pressed 30 µm into the skin: a receptor potential that sags a little, and spikes for as long as the press lasts.
  2. Ten times deeper, 300 µm: a bigger receptor potential and a faster rate, but only about three times the rate. The channels are running out.
  3. A Pacinian corpuscle: a burst when the press starts and another when it ends, nothing in between.
  4. A 250 Hz vibration only 5 µm deep: the Pacinian corpuscle fires once a cycle. Then try it on the Merkel ending: silence. This is how you feel a phone buzz.
  5. With TTX on the first node the receptor potential is still there. Turning force into current doesn’t need voltage-gated Na⁺ channels; only the spikes do.
  6. Press at a range of depths in one go, then choose what to plot against what.

Theory

In plain words: Pressing the skin stretches the membrane of a nerve ending, and stretch opens channels in it. They let positive ions in, mostly Na⁺, so the ending depolarizes. Press harder and more channels open: this depolarization, the receptor potential, grows with the press, the way an EPSP grows with the transmitter released.

A channel opened by force

The membrane of a touch ending holds mechanically gated channels. The main one in touch, and in the stretch receptors of muscle, is Piezo2: a large protein shaped like a three-bladed propeller that sits in the membrane and opens when the membrane around it is stretched. Mice without Piezo2 in their touch endings can hardly feel light touch at all.

Piezo2 is a cation channel: it lets through Na⁺, K⁺ and some Ca²⁺. Like the AMPA channel in The Chemical Synapse, a channel that passes both Na⁺ (battery +60 mV) and K⁺ (battery −90 mV) has a battery in between, near 0 mV. We call it Erec, the receptor channels’ reversal potential.

Ohm’s law, once more

Once open, the channels are one more conductance with its own battery. The current through them is their conductance times the driving force:

\[ I_\mathrm{rec} = g_\mathrm{rec}\,(V - E_\mathrm{rec}), \qquad g_\mathrm{rec} = g_\mathrm{mech}\,P_\mathrm{open}, \qquad E_\mathrm{rec} \approx 0\ \mathrm{mV} \]

gmech is the conductance if every channel were open and Popen the fraction open, which the press sets. At rest V is far below 0 mV, so the current is inward and depolarizes the ending. The resting conductance pulls back toward rest, and V settles where the two currents cancel: a weighted average of the two batteries, the same formula as for the resting voltage in Membrane Potential.

\[ V = \frac{g_\mathrm{rest}\,E_\mathrm{rest} + g_\mathrm{rec}\,E_\mathrm{rec}}{g_\mathrm{rest} + g_\mathrm{rec}} \]

A worked number (the Merkel ending of the simulation, pressed 30 µm): 37.5% of the channels open, so grec = 0.25 × 0.375 ≈ 0.094 mS/cm². With grest = 0.15 mS/cm² and Erest = −70 mV,

\[ V = \frac{0.15 \times (-70) + 0.094 \times 0}{0.15 + 0.094} \approx -43\ \mathrm{mV}, \]

a receptor potential of about 27 mV. The simulation gives about 25 mV at its peak: a little less, because the depolarization also opens a few of the node’s K⁺ channels, which pull back toward rest. The calculator below does this sum for any press.

Show the derivation

When V has stopped changing, no current charges the capacitor, so the receptor current in and the resting current out add to zero:

\[ g_\mathrm{rest}(V - E_\mathrm{rest}) + g_\mathrm{rec}(V - E_\mathrm{rec}) = 0 \]

Collect the terms with V:

\[ V\,(g_\mathrm{rest} + g_\mathrm{rec}) = g_\mathrm{rest}E_\mathrm{rest} + g_\mathrm{rec}E_\mathrm{rec} \]

and divide. Subtracting Erest from both sides gives the receptor potential itself:

\[ V - E_\mathrm{rest} = \frac{g_\mathrm{rec}}{g_\mathrm{rest} + g_\mathrm{rec}}\,\bigl(E_\mathrm{rec} - E_\mathrm{rest}\bigr) \]

The fraction in front is never more than 1, so the receptor potential can never be more than Erec − Erest, about 70 mV, however many channels open.

Graded, not all-or-none

The ending itself has few voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, so it does not fire: its voltage just follows the press, bigger for a bigger press and lasting as long as the channels stay open. That is why it is called a receptor (or generator) potential, and why it survives TTX, which blocks only the voltage-gated Na⁺ channels.

In plain words: The ending doesn’t fire. Its receptor current spreads to the first node of Ranvier on its axon, where there are plenty of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, and the node fires whenever it is pushed past threshold. The harder it is pushed, the sooner it gets back to threshold after each spike, so a bigger receptor potential means a higher firing rate.

Where the spike starts

The receptor current flows into the ending and spreads along the axon passively, as in Passive Properties: Space, shrinking with distance. The first node of Ranvier, where the myelin starts, is close enough to feel it, and it is packed with voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. It plays the part the axon initial segment plays in Summation and the Spike Trigger Zone: the place where the graded signal becomes spikes. In the simulation the ending and the node are lumped together as one patch of membrane, which is why the receptor potential chart is the node’s voltage with its Na⁺ channels blocked.

Threshold

The node fires once the receptor potential lifts it about 20 mV above rest (in the model, a press of about 16 µm on the Merkel ending). A light press gives a smaller receptor potential and no spikes at all: the brain hears nothing, however long it lasts.

More current, more spikes per second

After each spike the node’s voltage falls back below threshold, and the receptor current has to charge the membrane up again before the next spike. A bigger current charges it faster, so the spikes come closer together. This is the f–I relation of Firing Rate and Firing Patterns, with the current coming from the stimulus instead of an electrode. The spike train is the code: each spike is the same size, and the stimulus strength is in how often they come.

Show why a bigger current fires faster

Treat the node between spikes as the RC circuit of Passive Properties: Time. After a spike V starts again near rest, and the receptor current charges the membrane toward its steady value, rest plus the receptor potential ΔVrec, with time constant τ:

\[ V(t) - E_\mathrm{rest} = \Delta V_\mathrm{rec}\,\bigl(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\bigr) \]

It reaches threshold, ΔVth above rest, when

\[ t_\mathrm{th} = \tau \ln\frac{\Delta V_\mathrm{rec}}{\Delta V_\mathrm{rec} - \Delta V_\mathrm{th}} \]

so the rate is about 1/(tref + tth), with tref the refractory time after each spike. If ΔVrec is less than ΔVth the node never fires. Just above threshold tth is long and the rate low; the bigger ΔVrec, the shorter tth and the higher the rate. A real node also has K⁺ channels that stay open for a while after each spike and push V below rest, so it fires more slowly than this simple sum says, but the shape is the same.

Recording it

Adrian and Zotterman (1926) recorded from a single stretch receptor in a frog muscle: the spikes were all the same size, and pulling harder made them come faster. Katz (1950) then recorded the receptor potential itself, from a muscle spindle, and found it graded with the stretch and still there when the spikes were blocked.

In plain words: Doubling a light touch nearly doubles the response, but doubling a firm press adds only a little: most of the channels are already open, and the ending is already close to the channels’ battery. The response is compressed: over much of its range it grows roughly with the logarithm of the stimulus, so one axon can report presses from barely felt to firm.

Two things run out

The channels. The fraction open can’t go past 1. In the model it rises with the depth s of the press as

\[ P_\mathrm{open} = \frac{s}{s + s_{1/2}} \]

where s1/2 is the depth that opens half of them (50 µm for the Merkel ending). Ten times the press, from 30 to 300 µm, raises Popen from 0.375 to 0.857: not even 2.3 times.

The driving force. As the ending depolarizes, V gets closer to Erec, so each open channel passes less current. The receptor potential can never reach Erec − Erest, about 70 mV. In the simulation the receptor potential goes from about 25 to 33 mV for that tenfold press, and the late firing rate from about 53 to 160 spikes/s: three times, for ten times the press.

Show why the channel curve looks logarithmic

How steeply Popen rises for each step along a log axis is its slope against ln s:

\[ \frac{dP_\mathrm{open}}{d\ln s} = s\,\frac{dP_\mathrm{open}}{ds} = \frac{s\,s_{1/2}}{(s + s_{1/2})^2} \]

This is largest, 1/4, at s = s1/2, and changes slowly around it: from s1/2/3 to 3s1/2 it stays above 3/16, and Popen climbs from 0.25 to 0.75, nearly a straight line on a log axis. Far below s1/2, Popen ≈ s/s1/2 grows in proportion to s; far above, it hardly grows at all.

Why it helps

An axon can’t fire faster than a few hundred spikes a second, yet you can feel presses that differ a hundredfold or more. Compressing the scale, so that each tenfold step in the press adds a similar amount to the rate instead of multiplying it by ten, fits that range into the rates one axon can fire. (The model’s Merkel ending compresses even more than that above about 100 µm, where nearly all its channels are open.) It is the same compression as the decibel scale for sound, and it is why you notice a change in a stimulus by its ratio, not its size: adding one candle to a dark room is obvious, adding one to a lit stadium is not. A stronger stimulus also recruits more endings, and their axons together extend the range further.

In plain words: Some endings keep firing for as long as a stimulus lasts and report how strong it is (slowly adapting); others fire only when it changes (rapidly adapting) and report movement and vibration. A Pacinian corpuscle does its adapting mechanically: its onion of capsule layers passes on quick changes and soaks up steady pressure.

Slowly adapting: the Merkel ending

The firing rate is highest at the start of the press and then settles to a lower, steady rate that lasts as long as the press. In a real Merkel ending two cells share the work: the nerve ending’s own Piezo2 channels give the burst at the start, and the Merkel cell, which has Piezo2 too, keeps the ending firing through the press. The model lumps both into one set of channels, some of which close again over a few hundred milliseconds.

Rapidly adapting: the Pacinian capsule

The Pacinian corpuscle’s ending sits inside dozens of thin layers of capsule with fluid between them. Squeeze it slowly and the fluid has time to flow, the layers slide and settle, and the steady pressure is taken up by the outer layers; the core, with the ending, springs back almost to its rest shape. Only a quick change gets through to the core before the layers can settle. So the ending is squeezed when the press starts, again when it ends, and on every push of a vibration.

Peel the capsule away and the bare ending’s receptor potential lasts through a steady press: the capsule does most of the adapting (Loewenstein and Mendelson, 1965). The node adds some adaptation of its own, which this model leaves out.

Show the model’s capsule

The capsule is modelled as a slow copy of the press, xslow, that follows the actual depth x with a time constant τcap = 4 ms, like the voltage of the RC circuit following a step:

\[ \tau_\mathrm{cap}\,\frac{dx_\mathrm{slow}}{dt} = x - x_\mathrm{slow} \]

The ending feels only the part the capsule has not yet taken up, |x − xslow|. For a steady press this dies away within a few τcap; for a vibration much faster than 1/(2πτcap) ≈ 40 Hz, xslow can’t keep up and stays near the average depth, so nearly the whole vibration gets through. That is a high-pass filter, the same circuit as a capacitor in series with a resistor. Loewenstein and Skalak (1966) modelled the real capsule with springs and dashpots (shock absorbers) and found it acts as just such a filter.

The Pacinian ending in the model also has more sensitive channels (s1/2 = 5 µm instead of 50 µm) and a small, leaky first node (resting conductance 1 mS/cm², time constant about 1 ms), so it can fire again within each 4 ms cycle of a 250 Hz vibration.

Why have both?

Each kind reports something the other leaves out. Merkel endings tell you that a pen is still in your hand and how hard you hold it; Pacinian corpuscles tell you that it has just touched the paper and feel the vibration of the nib running over it. Mountcastle and his colleagues showed that the vibration a person can just feel at about 250 Hz matches what a Pacinian fibre can just detect.

In plain words: Every sense uses the same plan. The stimulus opens or closes channels in a receptor cell, the change in current makes a graded receptor potential, and spikes, somewhere along the way, carry the message to the brain. What differs is what opens the channels.

Direct and indirect

Touch, hearing and heat open their channels directly, within a fraction of a millisecond, which suits stimuli that change fast. Smell, sweet and bitter taste, and sight go through a receptor and a G protein first, the machinery of Slow Signals: Metabotropic Receptors and Neuromodulation. That is slower but amplifies enormously: one photon can close hundreds of channels in a rod.

Not every receptor cell fires

Hair cells, rods, cones and taste cells have no axon of their own. Their receptor potential changes how much transmitter they release onto a nerve fibre, as in The Chemical Synapse, and that fibre fires the spikes. Photoreceptors even run backwards: in the dark their channels are open and they release transmitter steadily; light closes the channels, hyperpolarizes them, and cuts the release.
