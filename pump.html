The Na⁺/K⁺ pump: keeping the batteries charged

The Membrane Potential lesson held the concentrations fixed: K⁺ high inside, Na⁺ high outside, and those differences are the batteries EK and ENa. But at rest Na⁺ leaks in and K⁺ leaks out all the time, and every spike lets in more. Left alone, the differences would run down and the batteries would go flat. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) keeps them charged: for every ATP it splits, it pushes 3 Na⁺ out and brings 2 K⁺ in. On this page the concentrations are free to change, and time runs in minutes.

You'll be able to
  • explain why a steady resting voltage needs a pump: the leaks never stop, so the concentrations are a balance, not an equilibrium
  • use Ohm’s law, I = g(V − E), to find each leak current, and the pump current that balances them
  • explain why the pump is electrogenic (3 charges out, 2 in) and how much that adds to the resting voltage
  • predict what blocking the pump (ouabain, digoxin, no ATP) does to [Na⁺]i, [K⁺]i, ENa, EK, V and the cell’s volume, and why it is slow, and faster in small cells
  • explain why a burst of spikes is followed by a hyperpolarization, and why the brain spends most of its energy on this pump

Simulation

Start here
  1. Leak in, pump out, exactly balanced: nothing changes. Yet in the Na⁺ traffic chart ions flow in and out all the time.
  2. Ouabain at 1 minute. V jumps about 10 mV at once (the pump’s own current is gone), then creeps up over an hour as Na⁺ builds up inside, K⁺ leaks away and the batteries ENa and EK shrink.
  3. 4,000 spikes at 50 Hz from 1 minute load the cell with Na⁺. The pump speeds up to clear it, and the cell sits about 7 mV more negative for many minutes.
  4. Ouabain again, in a cell of 1 µm radius instead of 10 µm: the same leak per square centimetre, but a tenth of the volume behind it, so it runs down ten times faster.
  5. [K⁺]o from 4 to 8 mM at 1 minute, as in hyperkalemia. EK rises and the cell depolarizes at once, to about −57 mV.
  6. Cl⁻ can cross now, and ouabain at 1 minute. Cl⁻ follows the Na⁺ in, water follows by osmosis, and the cell slowly swells, as brain cells do in a stroke.

Units

Real units

mM
millimolar, for concentration (millimoles per litre). Inside a neuron, about 12 mM Na⁺ and 140 mM K⁺; outside, about 145 mM Na⁺ and 4 mM K⁺.
mV
millivolts, for voltage. V is the inside measured relative to the outside.
min, s
minutes and seconds. Concentrations change over minutes; a spike lasts a millisecond.
µm
micrometres, for size. A neuron’s cell body is about 10–30 µm across; a thin unmyelinated axon is well under 1 µm.

For each square centimetre of membrane

µA/cm²
microamps per square centimetre, for current. The pump’s current here is 0.62 µA/cm² at rest; during a spike, the Na⁺ current reaches about a thousand times more, for a millisecond.
mS/cm²
millisiemens per square centimetre, for conductance. The resting leaks here are 0.014 (Na⁺) and 0.050 (K⁺); in the Hodgkin–Huxley lesson the Na⁺ conductance during a spike reaches tens. mS/cm² × mV = µA/cm².
µC/cm²
microcoulombs per square centimetre, for charge. Each spike here lets in 0.15 µC/cm² of Na⁺.
ATP/s
molecules of ATP split per second: one per pump cycle, and each cycle moves one net charge out, so 1 µA/cm² of pump current is 62,000 ATP per µm² of membrane each second.

Theory

In plain words: At rest, Na⁺ trickles in and K⁺ trickles out through the leak channels, all the time. The concentrations stay put only because the pump carries the same amounts back, using ATP. Think of a leaky boat with a bilge pump: the water level is steady because the pumping matches the leaking, not because nothing moves.

Each leak is Ohm’s law

As in the Membrane Potential lesson, each ion’s channels are a resistor (conductance g) in series with a battery (that ion’s Nernst potential E). The current through them is Ohm’s law: the conductance times the driving force, how far V is from that battery.

\[ I_\text{Na} = g_\text{Na}\,(V - E_\text{Na}), \qquad I_\text{K} = g_\text{K}\,(V - E_\text{K}) \]

A negative current is positive charge flowing in. At −70 mV, V is far below ENa, so Na⁺ flows in; V is a little above EK, so K⁺ flows out.

Worked number. In this cell ENa = +66.5 mV and EK = −94.9 mV. With gNa = 0.0136 mS/cm², INa = 0.0136 × (−70 − 66.5) = −1.86 µA/cm², inward. With gK = 0.0497 mS/cm², IK = 0.0497 × (−70 + 94.9) = +1.24 µA/cm², outward.

The pump pays it back

In the steady state the pump carries out exactly the Na⁺ that leaks in, and brings back exactly the K⁺ that leaks out. It moves 3 Na⁺ for every 2 K⁺, so if P is the pump current (one net charge out per cycle), the two leaks must be

\[ I_\text{Na} = -3P, \qquad I_\text{K} = +2P \]

Here P = 1.86 / 3 = 0.62 µA/cm², and then 2P = 1.24 µA/cm², exactly the K⁺ leak. The leaks must stand in the ratio 3 : 2, and that, more than anything, is what fixes where the resting voltage sits.

Show how fast the concentrations change

A current is charge per second, and Faraday’s constant F = 96,485 C/mol turns charge into moles. So a current density I (in A/cm²) of one ion carries I/F moles per second through each cm² of membrane. Spread over the volume behind that membrane, the concentration changes at

\[ \frac{d[\text{X}]_i}{dt} = -\frac{I_\text{X}}{F}\cdot\frac{A}{\text{Vol}}, \qquad \frac{A}{\text{Vol}} = \frac{4\pi r^2}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3} = \frac{3}{r}\ \text{for a sphere of radius } r. \]

For a cell body of radius 10 µm (10⁻³ cm), A/Vol = 3000 cm⁻¹, and 1 µA/cm² of one ion changes its concentration by 10⁻⁶ / 96,485 × 3000 = 3.1 × 10⁻⁸ mol/cm³ each second: 0.031 mM per second.

Without the pump, the Na⁺ leak of 1.86 µA/cm² would add 1.86 × 0.031 = 0.058 mM a second, about 3.5 mM a minute. [Na⁺]i starts at 12 mM, so it would nearly double in 4 minutes; [K⁺]i, losing as much K⁺ but starting at 140 mM, would drop by only a tenth. A thin axon of radius 0.5 µm has twenty times the area per volume, so the same leak changes it twenty times faster.

In plain words: Each cycle pushes three positive charges out and brings two back, so one net positive charge leaves. The pump is therefore a small, steady outward current. That current alone makes the inside a few millivolts more negative, but the pump’s bigger job is indirect: keeping the batteries ENa and EK where they are.

Add it to the balance of currents

At a steady voltage all the currents across the membrane add to zero (otherwise the membrane’s capacitor would keep charging). With the pump’s current included:

\[ g_\text{Na}(V - E_\text{Na}) + g_\text{K}(V - E_\text{K}) + I_\text{pump} = 0 \]

Solve for V:

\[ V = \frac{g_\text{Na}E_\text{Na} + g_\text{K}E_\text{K} - I_\text{pump}}{g_\text{Na} + g_\text{K}} \]

This is the chord-conductance equation from the Membrane Potential lesson with one more term. The first part is an average of the two batteries, each weighted by its conductance; the pump subtracts Ipump divided by the total conductance, which is just Ohm’s law, V = I/g.

Worked number. The total conductance is 0.0136 + 0.0497 = 0.0633 mS/cm². The pump’s share is 0.62 / 0.0633 = 9.8 mV. Without the pump, V would be (0.0136 × 66.5 + 0.0497 × (−94.9)) / 0.0633 = −60.2 mV; with it, −70.0 mV.

Why it is smaller in real neurons

Any other open channel adds to the total conductance and so shrinks the pump’s share. Cl⁻ channels are the main ones: tick Let Cl⁻ cross and the share drops from 9.8 to 7.0 mV. Rang and Ritchie saw this directly in thin nerve fibres: the hyperpolarization after a burst grew from a few millivolts to about 20 mV when they replaced the Cl⁻ outside with large anions that cannot cross. In most neurons the pump’s direct share of the resting voltage is a few millivolts.

Show why the share is about 10 mV, whatever the pump’s size

With only Na⁺ and K⁺ leaks, the steady state says gK(V − EK) = 2P and gNa(ENa − V) = 3P. So

\[ \frac{P}{g_\text{Na} + g_\text{K}} = \frac{1}{\dfrac{3}{E_\text{Na} - V} + \dfrac{2}{V - E_\text{K}}} = \frac{1}{\dfrac{3}{136.5} + \dfrac{2}{24.9}} = 9.8\ \text{mV} \]

P cancels: a bigger pump needs bigger leaks to balance it, and the bigger leaks shrink its effect just as much. The share is set by the 3 : 2 ratio and by where V sits between the batteries. Mullins and Noda wrote the same idea with the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation, putting the factor 3/2 in front of the K⁺ terms:

\[ V = \frac{RT}{F}\ln\frac{r[\text{K}^+]_o + b[\text{Na}^+]_o}{r[\text{K}^+]_i + b[\text{Na}^+]_i}, \qquad r = \tfrac{3}{2},\ \ b = \frac{P_\text{Na}}{P_\text{K}} \]

How to see the pump’s current

Block the pump suddenly and V jumps up by that share at once, long before any concentration has had time to change. A fast jump like this, followed by a slow drift, is the mark of an electrogenic pump.

In plain words: Block the pump and nothing collapses at once. Na⁺ creeps in and K⁺ creeps out, the batteries ENa and EK slowly shrink, and the cell depolarizes, faster and faster. How fast depends on the cell’s size: small cells and thin axons run down first. If Cl⁻ can cross, the cell also swells.

A fast part and a slow part

At once, the pump’s own current is gone: V jumps about 10 mV. Then, over tens of minutes, the concentrations run down. ENa falls first: [Na⁺]i starts small, so each millimole that leaks in is a big fraction of it. EK falls later, because [K⁺]i starts large.

Worked number (this cell, radius 10 µm). After 10 minutes without the pump, [Na⁺]i has gone from 12 to 37 mM and ENa from +66 to +36 mV, while EK has moved only from −95 to −90 mV. After 30 minutes V is about −57 mV, after an hour about −39 mV, and in about two hours both batteries are flat and V is near 0.

Small cells run down faster

The leak is set per square centimetre of membrane; the amount of Na⁺ and K⁺ is set by the volume behind it. A sphere has 3/r cm² of membrane per cm³, so a cell of radius 1 µm has ten times the membrane per volume of one of radius 10 µm, and runs down ten times faster. Thin unmyelinated axons (C fibres, 1 µm across or less) and fine dendrites depend on their pumps minute by minute.

Why the cell swells

Without the pump, Na⁺ in and K⁺ out swap roughly one for one, so the number of particles inside hardly changes and no water moves. But once V rises above ECl, Cl⁻ starts to flow in, and each Cl⁻ brings a positive ion with it to keep the charges balanced. More particles inside means water follows by osmosis, and the cell swells. So the pump has a quieter second job: by throwing Na⁺ back out as fast as it leaks in, it makes the membrane behave as if Na⁺ could not cross, and that keeps the cell’s volume steady. This is Tosteson and Hoffman’s “pump–leak” idea.

Show the osmotic bookkeeping

Water crosses the membrane quickly, so the cell keeps the total concentration of particles inside equal to the outside, here 298 mM. Inside at rest: 12 Na⁺ + 140 K⁺ + 8.7 Cl⁻ + 137 of large anions (proteins, phosphates) that are trapped inside = 298 mM. The trapped anions carry about −1 charge each, balancing the 152 − 8.7 = 143 mM of extra positive charge.

\[ \text{Vol} = \frac{\text{total amount of particles inside}}{298\ \text{mM}} \]

When the pump stops and Cl⁻ can cross, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ come in, the amount of particles grows, and so does the volume: in the simulation, by about 7 % in an hour and 25 % in two. With nothing to stop it, the trapped anions would keep drawing ions and water in (the Donnan effect) until the cell was several times its size.

In plain words: Every spike lets a little Na⁺ in and a little K⁺ out. One spike changes almost nothing, but thousands add up. The extra Na⁺ inside speeds up the pump, and the faster pump, being electrogenic, makes the cell more negative until it has cleared the Na⁺: a hyperpolarization that lasts minutes.

How much Na⁺ one spike lets in

The membrane is a capacitor of about 1 µF/cm². To swing it by about 100 mV takes 1 µF/cm² × 0.1 V = 0.1 µC/cm² of charge, the least Na⁺ a spike could let in. Real spikes let in more, because some Na⁺ channels are still open while the K⁺ channels open and let charge back out; Carter and Bean measured how much more in several kinds of neuron (from about a quarter more in pyramidal cells to more in fast-spiking ones). This simulation uses 0.15 µC/cm², and the same amount of K⁺ leaves.

Worked number. In a cell of radius 10 µm, 0.15 µC/cm² changes [Na⁺]i by 0.15 × 0.031 = 0.0047 mM. One spike: nothing. A burst of 4,000 spikes: 19 mM, taking [Na⁺]i from 12 mM to about 29 mM (the pump clears some during the 80 seconds of the burst).

The pump speeds up

The pump’s rate rises with the Na⁺ inside and the K⁺ outside, each saturating, like an enzyme with its substrate:

\[ I_\text{pump} = I_\text{max}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + \left(10\ \text{mM}/[\text{Na}^+]_i\right)^{1.5}}\cdot\frac{[\text{K}^+]_o}{[\text{K}^+]_o + 1.5\ \text{mM}} \]

At rest (12 mM Na⁺ inside, 4 mM K⁺ outside) it runs at 41 % of its top speed, Imax = 1.5 µA/cm². At 29 mM it runs at 60 %: half again as fast. Plot the pump current against [Na⁺]i after a burst to see the curve.

The post-tetanic hyperpolarization

The extra pump current, about 0.29 µA/cm², pushes V down by about 0.29 / 0.0633 = 4.6 mV (Ohm’s law again). The rest of the 7 mV comes from the batteries: ENa has shrunk, which pulls V down, more than the slightly shrunken EK pushes it up. Then, as the pump clears the Na⁺, everything returns over many minutes. In thin fibres, with far less volume per cm², it returns within a few minutes, and Rang and Ritchie found it needs K⁺ outside and is abolished by ouabain: it is the pump’s doing.

Show where the 7 mV comes from

At the lowest point, [Na⁺]i ≈ 29 mM and [K⁺]i ≈ 123 mM, so ENa = 26.7 ln(145/29) = +43.0 mV and EK = 26.7 ln(4/123) = −91.5 mV, and Ipump = 0.91 µA/cm². Change each term of V = (gNaENa + gKEK − Ipump)/(gNa + gK) in turn:

\[ \Delta V = \underbrace{\tfrac{0.0136}{0.0633}(-23.5)}_{E_\text{Na}\ \text{smaller}:\ -5.1} + \underbrace{\tfrac{0.0497}{0.0633}(+3.5)}_{E_\text{K}\ \text{smaller}:\ +2.7} \underbrace{-\ \tfrac{0.29}{0.0633}}_{\text{faster pump}:\ -4.6} \approx -7.0\ \text{mV} \]

In a real nerve, the K⁺ that leaves during the burst also piles up just outside the fibres, which pushes the other way; this simulation’s bath is big and stirred, so that is left out.

In plain words: Every pump cycle uses one ATP. The brain spends most of its energy pumping back the Na⁺ that its signals let in, so when its blood supply stops, the pump is the first thing to fail, and the batteries start to run down. Drugs and blood K⁺ levels that change the pump or EK matter for the heart as much as for nerves.

The cost

One net charge out per cycle, one ATP per cycle: 0.62 µA/cm² is 0.62 × 10⁻⁶ / 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.9 × 10¹² cycles per second per cm², about 39,000 per µm². This cell, with 1,260 µm² of membrane, splits about 49 million ATP each second just to stand still, and more when it fires. Attwell and Laughlin added up such costs for the grey matter of the brain and found that most of its signalling energy goes into pumping out the Na⁺ let in by spikes and synaptic currents. The brain is 2 % of the body’s weight but uses about a fifth of its energy at rest.

Stroke

Without blood flow there is no oxygen or glucose, ATP runs out within minutes, and the pumps stop, as if ouabain had been poured on every cell. Neurons depolarize, Na⁺, Cl⁻ and water flow in, and the cells swell: cytotoxic edema, which shows up on diffusion-weighted MRI and is one reason the swollen brain is dangerous after a large stroke.

Digoxin

Digoxin, from the foxglove plant, partly blocks the heart’s pumps. [Na⁺]i rises a little, so the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger, which uses the Na⁺ gradient to push Ca²⁺ out, removes less Ca²⁺; more Ca²⁺ is stored, and each beat is stronger. Too much digoxin and the cells depolarize, causing arrhythmias, and K⁺ builds up in the blood because the pumps everywhere take up less of it.

Potassium in the blood

Hyperkalemia (high K⁺ outside) raises EK and depolarizes nerve and heart cells, at first making them more excitable, then less as their Na⁺ channels inactivate (the Action Potential lesson). Hypokalemia does the reverse and also slows the pump, which needs K⁺ outside to bind.

Worked number. Doubling [K⁺]o from 4 to 8 mM moves EK by 26.7 × ln 2 = 18.5 mV, from −94.9 to −76.4 mV. V jumps to about −57 mV at once. The pump speeds up a little (it binds K⁺ outside better), lowers [Na⁺]i from 12 to 9 mM over the next quarter of an hour, and V drifts up a few more millivolts.
