Sending a signal: the action potential travels

A neuron has to get a message from one end to the other, sometimes a metre away, from your spinal cord to your toes. It does it with an action potential: a spike of voltage that regenerates itself as it moves along the axon. Fire one, block it, and change the axon, then explain what you see with the theory below, using what you learned in lessons 1 and 2.

You'll be able to
  • build the spike from lesson 1's ideas: Na⁺ current in and K⁺ current out, each set by its driving force V − E (Nernst), with the membrane's capacitor in between (lesson 2: IC = C dV/dt)
  • explain why a spike is all-or-none: past threshold, Na⁺ coming in beats K⁺ going out, and more Na⁺ channels open
  • explain how it travels: current spreads ahead inside the axon and charges the next patch of membrane, with the same time constant τ as in lesson 2, until that patch reaches threshold too
  • explain the cell body's part: it is a big capacitor that has to charge (τ, lesson 2) before the spike can start in the axon
  • predict how axon diameter and myelin change the conduction velocity

What you already have

From lesson 1: each ion's channels act like a resistor in series with a battery, the Nernst potential E. The current is Ohm's law with the driving force V − E:

\[ I_\mathrm{Na} = g_\mathrm{Na}\,(V - E_\mathrm{Na}), \qquad I_\mathrm{K} = g_\mathrm{K}\,(V - E_\mathrm{K}) \]

With lesson 1's mammalian concentrations at body temperature, EK ≈ −89 mV, ENa ≈ +62 mV and ECl ≈ −71 mV (a little larger than lesson 1's numbers at 25 °C, because RT/F grows with temperature). At rest the K⁺ and Cl⁻ channels are open far more than the Na⁺ channels, so a bare axon sits near −73 mV; a node of Ranvier, with many K⁺ leak channels, sits closer to EK, near −84 mV. This is the chord-conductance equation of lesson 1:

\[ V_\mathrm{rest} = \frac{g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}E_\mathrm{Cl}}{g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}} \]

From lesson 2: the lipid bilayer is a capacitor, and the current that doesn't leave through the channels charges it:

\[ C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = -\,(I_\mathrm{Na} + I_\mathrm{K} + I_\mathrm{Cl}) + I_\mathrm{stim} \]

What is new

During a spike gNa jumps far above gK, so the same equation says V heads toward ENa; then gK takes over and V heads back toward EK. The spike is the chord-conductance balance tipping one way and then the other.

The conductances are no longer fixed. Na⁺ and K⁺ channels open and close depending on the voltage itself, so a change in V changes g, which changes the currents, which changes V. That loop makes the action potential. The rest of the lesson takes it in two steps: one patch (what a patch does over time), then many patches in a row (what travels).

Three kinds of gate

  • Na⁺ activation gate (m): opens quickly when the inside becomes less negative.
  • Na⁺ inactivation gate (h): closes more slowly when the inside becomes less negative, and shuts the channel again even though the activation gate is still open.
  • K⁺ gate (n): opens slowly when the inside becomes less negative.

A Na⁺ channel conducts only when it is activated and not inactivated; the conductances are \(g_\mathrm{Na}\,m^3h\) and \(g_\mathrm{K}\,n^4\).

The spike, step by step

1. Threshold. A stimulus pushes V up a little. Some Na⁺ channels open, and Na⁺ flows in because V is far below ENa. That inward current pushes V up more. If the stimulus was too weak, the outward K⁺ and leak currents win, and V falls back with the time constant τ. If it was strong enough, the inward Na⁺ current wins and the loop runs away: positive feedback. This is why a spike is all-or-none.

2. Upstroke. V rushes toward ENa. It never reaches it, because the K⁺ and leak conductances pull the other way and the driving force on Na⁺ shrinks as V approaches ENa.

3. Downstroke. Two slower things now happen: h falls, shutting the Na⁺ channels, and n rises, opening K⁺ channels. Na⁺ current stops and K⁺ current carries V back down.

4. Undershoot. The K⁺ channels are slow to close, so for a few ms V dips closer to EK than at rest, until n falls back.

Why does the voltage run away at threshold?

Near rest, write the total ionic current as a function of V. Where \(dI/dV\) of the instantaneous current is positive, the circuit is stable: a small push in V is met by a current that pushes back. Opening Na⁺ channels adds a negative slope (more depolarization gives more inward current), and when this negative slope is larger than the positive slope of the K⁺ and leak conductances, the net current points the same way as the push. That crossing point is the threshold.

Inputs arrive at the cell body

In a real neuron the inputs from other cells arrive on the dendrites and cell body, as small currents through synaptic channels. They are too small and too slow to start a spike on their own, and the cell body has few voltage-gated channels. What happens to them is the RC circuit of lesson 2: the cell body is a big capacitor, and the input current has to charge it.

\[ \tau = \frac{C}{\Sigma g}, \qquad C = c_m \times (\text{membrane area}) \]

A bigger cell body has more membrane area, so more capacitance. The same current moves its voltage more slowly. Part of the current also leaks into the axon.

Where the spike starts

The charge that builds up on the cell body flows into the nearest piece of axon, which has the voltage-gated channels. That piece reaches threshold first, and its spike is then what charges the next piece. The region where this happens, the axon hillock and the start of the axon (the initial segment), is the trigger zone of the neuron: the place where all the inputs are added up and where the decision to fire is made.

The spike goes down the axon, and also back into the cell body, which you can see on the grey trace in the Voltage at the electrodes chart.

The patch next door

When one patch fires, its inside becomes positive. The inside of the axon is a continuous salty fluid, so current flows through it, along the axon, from the active patch toward the neighbouring patches that are still at rest. This is Ohm's law again: the cytoplasm is a resistor, and the voltage difference between two patches drives the current.

That current goes into the next patch and has to go somewhere. Part of it leaves through that patch's leak channels, and the rest charges its capacitor, exactly as in lesson 2: \(I_C = C\,dV/dt\). The patch's voltage rises with the time constant \(\tau = C/\Sigma g\). If it rises past threshold, its own Na⁺ channels open and it fires, and the process repeats for the patch after it. The spike travels because each patch is a fresh amplifier for the next.

How far does passive spread go?

With no voltage-gated channels (set gNa to 0) the spread dies away with distance, because current leaks out through the membrane all along the way. A steady change in V falls off as

\[ V(x) = V_0\, e^{-x/\lambda}, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{r_m}{r_i}} \]

where \(r_m\) is the resistance of the membrane leak per unit length (higher is better, current stays inside) and \(r_i\) is the resistance of the cytoplasm per unit length (lower is better, current flows easily along). λ is the distance over which V falls to 37% of its starting value. The spike is regenerated all along the way, so it does not fade, but λ and τ set how quickly each patch gets charged to threshold, and so how fast the spike travels.

Where the length constant comes from

Take a short piece of axon of length \(dx\). Current enters from the left through the cytoplasm, current leaves to the right, and the difference leaves through the membrane (resistive and capacitive parts). The voltage drop along the cytoplasm is \(dV = -I_i\, r_i\,dx\) (Ohm's law). The current leaving through the membrane per unit length is \(V/r_m + c_m\,\partial V/\partial t\). Putting these together gives the cable equation

\[ \frac{1}{r_i}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} = \frac{V}{r_m} + c_m \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \]

For a steady voltage the time term is zero, leaving \(\lambda^2\,V'' = V\) with \(\lambda^2 = r_m/r_i\), whose decaying solution is \(e^{-x/\lambda}\). The time term is lesson 2's capacitor, and the space term is new. That is the equation the simulation solves, one short piece of axon at a time.

Why the spike only goes forward

The current from an active patch flows to both neighbours, so why doesn't the spike also run backward? Because the patch it just came from is refractory: its Na⁺ channels are inactivated (h is low) and its K⁺ channels are still open (n is high). That patch cannot fire again for a few ms, whatever the current it receives. The only neighbour that can fire is the one ahead.

Why it matters

  • It keeps signals moving in one direction along the axon.
  • It limits how often a neuron can fire, which is the highest firing rate of the cell.
  • It explains why a local anaesthetic or TTX that blocks Na⁺ channels stops the signal: with no inward current there is nothing to regenerate.

A spike started in the middle of an axon does travel both ways, because there is no refractory patch on either side to begin with. Stimulate at B or C instead of A to see it: one spike runs on to the terminals and another runs back toward the cell body. Whether that one gets in depends on the cell body's size. A big cell body is a large capacitor for the thin axon to charge, so on the motor neuron the backward spike stalls at the start of the axon and the cell body hardly moves; shrink the Cell body slider to 20 µm and it gets through and fires the cell body. Real motor neurons show the same thing. Stimulate at two electrodes at once and the spikes running toward each other meet and stop, each running into the other's refractory wake.

Diameter

A wider axon has a wider cytoplasm, so \(r_i\) is smaller and current spreads farther ahead of the spike. The patch ahead charges faster, so the spike moves faster. The membrane area (and so the capacitance and leak) grows with the diameter too, so the net effect is that velocity rises with the square root of the diameter, \(v \propto \sqrt{d}\). Squid have giant axons for their escape jet for this reason. But it is an expensive way to be fast: a human nerve full of squid-sized axons would be metres wide.

Myelin

Wrapping the axon in insulation makes the membrane act like a much thicker one: the capacitance falls and the leak falls, both by a large factor. Less capacitance means less charge to move to change V, so each patch charges faster (smaller τ for the same current). Less leak means a much larger λ, so current spreads much farther before it fades. Na⁺ channels sit only at the bare nodes, and the spike jumps from node to node, called saltatory conduction. The nodes are the only places where energy is spent on pumping ions back out, so myelin is cheaper too.

On the motor neuron here, taking the myelin off the same 12 µm axon slows it from about 80 m/s to about 7 m/s. Thinner myelin is slower too, and below about 25 wraps the current leaks away through the sheath before the next node reaches threshold, so the spike stops. This is why a 12 µm myelinated axon is faster than a half-millimetre squid axon, and why diseases that damage myelin (multiple sclerosis, Guillain–Barré syndrome) slow or stop conduction: a bare internode has almost no Na⁺ channels, so the current from the last node leaks away charging it.

How the simulation models myelin

Internodes are 100 axon diameters long (1.2 mm for a 12 µm axon), with the capacitance and leak of bare membrane divided by 2 × the number of wraps (set with the Myelin wraps slider; 100 wraps, the default, is 0.5%) and no voltage-gated channels. Nodes are 1 µm long, with 20 times the squid axon's Na⁺ channels and always-open K⁺ channels, roughly as real mammalian nodes have. The first 30 µm of axon, the initial segment, is built like a node.

Where these ideas come from

The original papers behind this lesson, oldest first within each idea. Most are free to read through the DOI links.

  1. Hodgkin AL, Huxley AF (1939). Action potentials recorded from inside a nerve fibre. Nature 144:710–711. doi:10.1038/144710a0
    The first recording from inside an axon: the spike overshoots zero, so the inside briefly becomes positive.
  2. Hodgkin AL, Katz B (1949). The effect of sodium ions on the electrical activity of the giant axon of the squid. Journal of Physiology 108:37–77. doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1949.sp004310
    The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz voltage equation, and the sodium hypothesis: the spike is a brief rise in Na⁺ permeability.
  3. Hodgkin AL, Huxley AF (1952). A quantitative description of membrane current and its application to conduction and excitation in nerve. Journal of Physiology 117:500–544. doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1952.sp004764
    The membrane as Na⁺ and K⁺ conductances with Nernst batteries and a capacitor, the gates m, h and n, and a computed travelling spike. The channels in lesson 3 are theirs, including the 3× speed-up per 10 °C.
  4. Hodgkin AL, Rushton WAH (1946). The electrical constants of a crustacean nerve fibre. Proceedings of the Royal Society of London B 133:444–479. doi:10.1098/rspb.1946.0024
    Cable theory for an axon: membrane resistance and capacitance, the time constant τ and the length constant λ.
  5. Hursh JB (1939). Conduction velocity and diameter of nerve fibers. American Journal of Physiology 127:131–139. doi:10.1152/ajplegacy.1939.127.1.131
    In myelinated fibres, conduction velocity rises in proportion to fibre diameter (about 6 m/s per µm).
  6. Tasaki I (1939). The electro-saltatory transmission of the nerve impulse and the effect of narcosis upon the nerve fiber. American Journal of Physiology 127:211–227. doi:10.1152/ajplegacy.1939.127.2.211
    Saltatory conduction: the impulse jumps from node to node and can skip a blocked node.
  7. Huxley AF, Stämpfli R (1949). Evidence for saltatory conduction in peripheral myelinated nerve fibres. Journal of Physiology 108:315–339. doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1949.sp004335
    Current measured at nodes and internodes: the spike regenerates only at the nodes of Ranvier.
  8. Rushton WAH (1951). A theory of the effects of fibre size in medullated nerve. Journal of Physiology 115:101–122. doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1951.sp004655
    Why velocity grows with diameter in myelinated fibres but only with its square root in bare ones.
  9. Narahashi T, Moore JW, Scott WR (1964). Tetrodotoxin blockage of sodium conductance increase in lobster giant axons. Journal of General Physiology 47:965–974. doi:10.1085/jgp.47.5.965
    Tetrodotoxin (TTX) blocks the Na⁺ conductance and leaves the K⁺ conductance alone.
  10. Coombs JS, Curtis DR, Eccles JC (1957). The interpretation of spike potentials of motoneurones. Journal of Physiology 139:198–231. doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1957.sp005887
    In motor neurons the spike starts in the initial segment of the axon, and one arriving backwards up the axon can fail to invade the large cell body.
  11. Ritchie JM, Rogart RB (1977). Density of sodium channels in mammalian myelinated nerve fibers and nature of the axonal membrane under the myelin sheath. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA 74:211–215. doi:10.1073/pnas.74.1.211
    Na⁺ channels are packed at the nodes of Ranvier, about 12,000 per µm², with very few under the myelin.
  12. Nernst W (1889). Die elektromotorische Wirksamkeit der Ionen. Zeitschrift für physikalische Chemie 4:129–181.
    The Nernst equation: the voltage at which an ion's concentration difference is balanced.
