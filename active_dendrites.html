Active dendrites: the cable fights back

Passive Properties: Space treated a dendrite as a leaky cable: every input shrinks and slows on its way to the cell body, and inputs simply add. Real dendrites also carry voltage-gated channels, so the cable can push back. A spike in the cell body runs back out into the dendrites, kept going by their Na⁺ channels. A handful of inputs close together on one thin branch can set off a local spike of their own, through NMDA receptors. And HCN channels, open at rest, keep far-away inputs brief. Some of the adding-up happens in the dendrites, before the cell body sees anything.

You'll be able to
  • explain the back-propagating action potential, and why the dendrites' Na⁺ channels keep it from dying out
  • explain why inputs clustered on one thin branch can add up to more than their sum: their NMDA receptors lose their Mg²⁺ block
  • use Ohm's law, I = g (V − E), to see why a current that grows as the voltage rises feeds on itself
  • explain what HCN channels (Ih) do: open at rest, they shorten far-off EPSPs and stop them piling up
  • contrast a passive cable's shrinking, simply added inputs with an active dendrite's

Theory

In plain words: In Passive Properties: Space each short piece of dendrite was a patch of membrane, a resistor with its battery (the leak) plus a capacitor, joined to its neighbours by the resistance of the cytoplasm. Here each piece just gets more resistors: one for each kind of channel, each with its own battery, and some of them change with the voltage, as in The Hodgkin–Huxley Model. Each one still obeys Ohm's law: its current is its conductance times its driving force, I = g (V − E).

One piece of dendrite

For piece \(k\), current arriving along the cytoplasm from its two neighbours either charges its capacitor or leaves through its channels:

\[ C\,\frac{dV_k}{dt} = g_c\,(V_{k-1} - V_k) + g_c\,(V_{k+1} - V_k) - \sum_\text{channels} g_x(V_k, t)\,(V_k - E_x) \]

With every \(g_x\) fixed, this is the passive cable and its length constant λ. The new part is the sign of each channel's feedback. If a conductance grows as V rises and its battery is above V (Na⁺ channels, NMDA receptors), the current it lets in pushes V up further: the dendrite amplifies. If a conductance shrinks as V rises while its battery is above V (HCN channels), rising V turns off inward current: the dendrite damps.

A quick number: HCN channels at rest

350 µm out, the dendrite has \(\bar g_h \approx 0.24\) mS/cm² of HCN channels, of which about 7% are open at its resting −61 mV: \(g_h \approx 0.017\) mS/cm². Their driving force is \(V - E_h = -61 - (-30) = -31\) mV, so by Ohm's law \(I_h = 0.017 \times (-31) \approx -0.5\ \mu\text{A/cm}^2\): a small, steady inward current. Across the whole cell it holds rest about 8 mV above where it would otherwise be (−70 mV, the leak's battery).

Show the derivation: how the page solves the cell

Each dendrite is cut into 60 pieces 10 µm long, each with the equation above. The page steps time forward by 0.025 ms (0.05 ms in part 3), writing every piece's equation at the end of the step (implicitly), so the voltages in a dendrite form a chain of equations that can be solved exactly, from the tip inward (the Thomas algorithm). The cell body's equation joins the 20 chains. The gates move by the same steps, with Traub and Miles' rates for the Na⁺ and K⁺ channels, in the form Pospischil and colleagues used for cortical neurons.

Dendrites that receive the same inputs behave identically, so the page solves one of each kind and counts its current into the cell body as many times as there are of them. With inputs on 6 of the 20 dendrites, it solves one dendrite with an input (×6) and one without (×14). This is exact, not an approximation: spread inputs really are simulated on separate dendrites, and a run takes a few tens of milliseconds.

In plain words: When the cell fires, its cell body's inside suddenly becomes about 90 mV more positive than the dendrites'. By Ohm's law current flows along the cytoplasm from high to low, out into the dendrites, and charges them. In a passive dendrite that is all, and a quick spike fades fast. With Na⁺ channels in the dendrite, each stretch the spike reaches opens its own Na⁺ channels and adds inward current, boosting the spike as it goes. Dendrites have fewer Na⁺ channels than an axon, so the spike is boosted, not fully rebuilt, and still shrinks with distance.

Where it starts and where it goes

A spike starts in the axon initial segment, where Na⁺ channels are densest (Summation and the Spike Trigger Zone). It runs forward down the axon, and it also spreads backwards, through the cell body and out into the dendrites. Stuart and Sakmann showed this in 1994 by recording from the cell body and the apical dendrite of the same neuron at once: the spike reached the dendrite later and smaller, and TTX on the dendrite made it much smaller still.

A quick number

The dendrites here are 1.2 µm thick, with membrane resistance \(R_m = 1/(0.05\ \text{mS/cm}^2) = 20\,000\ \Omega\,\text{cm}^2\) and cytoplasm \(R_i = 150\ \Omega\,\text{cm}\). As in Passive Properties: Space:

\[ \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{d\,R_m}{4\,R_i}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.2\times10^{-4}\ \text{cm} \times 20\,000\ \Omega\,\text{cm}^2}{600\ \Omega\,\text{cm}}} \approx 0.063\ \text{cm} = 632\ \mu\text{m} \]

A steady voltage change would keep \(e^{-300/632} \approx 62\%\) of its size 300 µm out. The spike, with the dendrites' Na⁺ channels blocked, keeps only about 17% there in the simulation. A spike lasts about 1 ms, much less than the membrane's time constant (\(\tau = R_m C = 20\) ms), so most of the current that flows out is spent charging the capacitance of the first stretch of dendrite, and little gets farther: a quick signal fades faster than a steady one. With ḡNa = 7 mS/cm², about two thirds of the spike reaches 300–400 µm.

Show the derivation: why a quick signal fades faster

The cable equation of Passive Properties: Space, with V measured from rest, is

\[ \lambda^2\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} = V + \tau\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \]

For a voltage that wobbles in time as \(e^{i\omega t}\) (frequency \(f = \omega/2\pi\)), \(\partial V/\partial t = i\omega V\), and the equation becomes \(\lambda^2 V'' = (1 + i\omega\tau)\,V\). Its solution falls with distance as \(e^{-x/\lambda_f}\), with

\[ \lambda_f = \frac{\lambda}{\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{1 + i\,\omega\tau}} \]

For a steady change (\(\omega = 0\)) this is λ. A 1 ms spike is made mostly of frequencies around 300 Hz: \(\omega\tau = 2\pi \times 300\ \text{s}^{-1} \times 0.02\ \text{s} \approx 38\), and \(\sqrt{1 + 38i} \approx 4.4 + 4.3i\). So \(\lambda_f \approx 632/4.4 \approx 144\ \mu\text{m}\), and \(e^{-300/144} \approx 12\%\) is left at 300 µm: the same range as the simulation's 17% (whose dendrites are finite and sealed at the tip, which holds a little more voltage).

Why it matters

The back-propagating spike tells every synapse on the dendrites that the cell has just fired. Its depolarization adds to a recent EPSP and helps drive Mg²⁺ out of that synapse's NMDA receptors, letting Ca²⁺ in: the coincidence that triggers LTP in Spines and Plasticity. Because it shrinks with distance (and shrinks more when the cell fires fast, as the dendrites' Na⁺ channels inactivate), synapses far out hear less of it.

In plain words: An NMDA receptor's pore is plugged by a Mg²⁺ ion at rest, and the plug falls out as the inside becomes more positive. One input on a branch depolarizes it only a little. Ten inputs on the same thin branch depolarize it a lot, which unplugs their NMDA receptors, which lets in more current, which depolarizes it more: positive feedback, like Na⁺ channels at threshold, but slower and local to the branch. Spread the same inputs over many branches and none of them depolarizes enough to start it.

The Mg²⁺ block

The share of NMDA receptors free of Mg²⁺ depends on the voltage and on the Mg²⁺ concentration outside. The page uses Jahr and Stevens' fit (V in mV):

\[ B(V) = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{[\text{Mg}^{2+}]}{3.57\ \text{mM}}\; e^{-0.062\,V}}, \qquad I_\text{NMDA} = \bar g_\text{NMDA}(t)\; B(V)\,(V - 0) \]

The second part is Ohm's law: the receptor's battery is at 0 mV, and its conductance is the open receptors' conductance times the share that are unplugged.

A quick number

With 1 mM Mg²⁺: at −70 mV, \(e^{4.34}/3.57 \approx 21.5\), so \(B \approx 1/22.5 \approx 4.4\%\). At −20 mV, \(e^{1.24}/3.57 \approx 0.97\), so \(B \approx 51\%\): more than eleven times as many receptors conduct. Take ten inputs' NMDA receptors, 15 nS when all are unplugged. At −70 mV: \(g = 15 \times 0.044 \approx 0.67\) nS and \(I = 0.67 \times (-70 - 0) \approx -47\) pA. At −20 mV: \(g \approx 7.6\) nS and \(I \approx 7.6 \times (-20) \approx -152\) pA. The driving force has shrunk to less than a third, yet the inward current has more than tripled. That is the positive feedback.

Why a thin branch, and why all on one

By Ohm's law the depolarization an input causes is its current times the resistance it meets, ΔV = I × R. Far out on a thin dendrite, current has few places to go, so R is high: in the simulation one input at 300 µm gives about 2 mV where it lands but only 0.16 mV at the cell body. Ten inputs on the same spot add their currents into that one high resistance, the branch climbs past the point where the feedback takes over, and it jumps to about −25 mV, where it stays while glutamate holds the receptors open (tens of ms). Spread one input to each of ten branches and each branch only gets its own 2 mV: the receptors stay plugged, and the cell body sees ten small bumps simply added. So where inputs land, not just how many, changes what the cell does: each thin branch can act as a small threshold unit of its own (Polsky, Mel and Schiller, 2004).

Show the derivation: a current that grows as the driving force shrinks

How the NMDA current changes with voltage is its slope, \(dI/dV\). Differentiating \(I = \bar g\,B(V)\,V\), and using \(dB/dV = 0.062\,B\,(1 - B)\) (which follows from the fit above):

\[ \frac{dI_\text{NMDA}}{dV} = \bar g\,B\,\bigl[\,1 + 0.062\,V\,(1 - B)\,\bigr] \]

The bracket is negative whenever \(V\,(1 - B) < -16\) mV, which with 1 mM Mg²⁺ holds from rest up to about −27 mV. There the current becomes more inward as V rises: a negative slope. Every other current in the branch (the leak, the cell body pulling back along the dendrite) has a positive slope and opposes it. When the inputs' \(\bar g\) is small, the positive slopes win and the branch settles near rest; when it is large enough, the total slope turns negative and any push sets off the run-up, which stops only where the NMDA current's slope turns positive again, near −25 mV. In plain words: HCN channels work backwards from most voltage-gated channels: they open when the voltage goes down, and close, slowly, when it goes up. Their battery is near −30 mV, so at rest the few that are open let in a small inward current, Ih. An EPSP far out depolarizes the membrane and, over tens of ms, closes some of them. Losing inward current pulls the voltage back down, cutting the EPSP short, so far-off EPSPs stay brief and pile up less.

Two effects

  • At rest, the open HCN channels are extra conductance, a leakier membrane. Far out (350 µm) they add about 0.017 mS/cm² to the leak's 0.05, so the membrane's time constant falls from about 20 ms to \(1/(0.05 + 0.017) \approx 15\) ms.
  • During an EPSP, they close slowly (here with a time constant of 40 ms), so the inward current they carry shrinks just as the EPSP should be decaying. That acts like an extra outward current arriving late: it does little to the EPSP's rise but pulls its tail down sooner.

In many pyramidal cells HCN channels get denser with distance from the cell body; Magee (1998) measured more than a sixfold rise along CA1 dendrites. Far inputs, which a passive dendrite would spread out and slow down the most, get cut short the most. In the simulation, five inputs 25 ms apart pile up about equally whether they land at 50 or 350 µm, as long as Ih is working; block it and the far ones pile up more. Magee (1999) found the same in CA1 neurons: Ih makes how inputs add up in time independent of where they land.

Show the derivation: a current that switches itself off

The HCN current obeys Ohm's law, with q the share of channels open:

\[ I_h = \bar g_h\, q\, (V - E_h), \qquad \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{q_\infty(V) - q}{\tau_q}, \qquad q_\infty(V) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(V + 82)/8}} \]

Near rest, \(V - E_h \approx -32\) mV: \(I_h\) is inward. When an EPSP raises V, \(q_\infty\) falls and q follows it over \(\tau_q \approx 40\) ms. The change in current is \(\Delta I_h \approx \bar g_h\,(V - E_h)\,\Delta q\): a negative driving force times a negative \(\Delta q\), a positive (outward) change. It grows over tens of ms, so it arrives as the EPSP is falling, and pulls it down. Block the channels and both effects go: the membrane is less leaky, rest drops to the leak's −70 mV, and nothing hurries the EPSPs' tails.

In plain words: A neuron is not a bag that simply adds up its inputs. Its dendrites amplify some inputs and damp others, depending on where they land and when, and they tell their synapses when the cell has fired. That shapes what a neuron computes, how its synapses learn, and how several common drugs act.

Learning

A synapse strengthens (LTP) when its NMDA receptors pass enough Ca²⁺, which takes glutamate and depolarization at once. The back-propagating spike supplies the depolarization when the cell fires just after the synapse: input, then output, strengthens the input (Spines and Plasticity). An NMDA spike can supply it too, without the cell firing at all, which may let inputs that arrive together on one branch strengthen together.

Computing

Inputs on the same thin branch combine in an S-shaped way (little, then a jump, then a ceiling), while inputs on different branches simply add. A pyramidal neuron with dozens of thin branches behaves somewhat like a two-layer network: many small threshold units, the branches, feeding one big one, the cell body and axon.

Medicine

  • Memantine (used in Alzheimer's disease) and ketamine (an anaesthetic; esketamine is used for depression) block NMDA receptors by lodging in the open channel, near where Mg²⁺ sits.
  • Ivabradine, used in heart failure, blocks HCN channels in the heart's pacemaker cells, where their current is called If (the "funny" current), and so slows the heart. The same family of channels makes Ih in dendrites.
  • Mg²⁺ itself: in Mg²⁺-free solution NMDA receptors are unblocked at every voltage (set [Mg²⁺] to 0 in the calculator), and brain slices bathed in it tend to produce seizure-like bursts.

  2. Mayer ML, Westbrook GL, Guthrie PB (1984). Voltage-dependent block by Mg²⁺ of NMDA responses in spinal cord neurones. Nature 309:261–263. doi:10.1038/309261a0The same discovery, made at the same time: without Mg²⁺, NMDA responses lose their voltage dependence.
  3. Jahr CE, Stevens CF (1990). Voltage dependence of NMDA-activated macroscopic conductances predicted by single-channel kinetics. Journal of Neuroscience 10:3178–3182. doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.10-09-03178.1990The equation for the Mg²⁺ block used here.
  4. Stuart GJ, Sakmann B (1994). Active propagation of somatic action potentials into neocortical pyramidal cell dendrites. Nature 367:69–72. doi:10.1038/367069a0The back-propagating spike, recorded from the cell body and the dendrite of the same neuron.
  5. Magee JC (1998). Dendritic hyperpolarization-activated currents modify the integrative properties of hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons. Journal of Neuroscience 18:7613–7624. doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.18-19-07613.1998Ih rises more than sixfold from the cell body out along the dendrite, and shortens EPSPs.
  6. Magee JC (1999). Dendritic Ih normalizes temporal summation in hippocampal CA1 neurons. Nature Neuroscience 2:508–514. doi:10.1038/9158With Ih, near and far inputs pile up alike in time; blocking it makes far inputs pile up more.
  7. Schiller J, Major G, Koester HJ, Schiller Y (2000). NMDA spikes in basal dendrites of cortical pyramidal neurons. Nature 404:285–289. doi:10.1038/35005094Clustered inputs on a thin basal dendrite set off local spikes carried mainly by NMDA receptors.
  8. Häusser M, Spruston N, Stuart GJ (2000). Diversity and dynamics of dendritic signaling. Science 290:739–744. doi:10.1126/science.290.5492.739Review: the channels in dendrites and what they do.
  9. Polsky A, Mel BW, Schiller J (2004). Computational subunits in thin dendrites of pyramidal cells. Nature Neuroscience 7:621–627. doi:10.1038/nn1253Inputs on one branch combine in an S-shaped way; inputs on different branches add linearly.
  10. Pospischil M, Toledo-Rodriguez M, Monier C, Piwkowska Z, Bal T, Frégnac Y, Markram H, Destexhe A (2008). Minimal Hodgkin–Huxley type models for different classes of cortical and thalamic neurons. Biological Cybernetics 99:427–441. doi:10.1007/s00422-008-0263-8The Na⁺ and K⁺ channels' rates used in this model (Traub and Miles' kinetics).
  11. Major G, Larkum ME, Schiller J (2013). Active properties of neocortical pyramidal neuron dendrites. Annual Review of Neuroscience 36:1–24. doi:10.1146/annurev-neuro-062111-150343Review: back-propagating spikes, NMDA spikes and other dendritic spikes in cortical neurons.
