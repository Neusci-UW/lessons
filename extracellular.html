Recording from outside: extracellular spikes and the compound action potential

Every lesson so far put an electrode inside the cell. In the lab, in the clinic and in the brain, most recordings are made from outside: a wire beside a neuron, hooks under a nerve, pads on the skin over a muscle, discs on the scalp. Outside, you don't see the voltage across the membrane. You see the small voltage drops made by current flowing through the fluid around the cells, and that changes what a spike looks like. This is what you will record in the lab with Newt.

You'll be able to
  • explain with Ohm's law why a spike recorded from outside is tiny (microvolts) and shaped like the current crossing the membrane, not like the voltage inside
  • explain why it shrinks with distance, so an electrode only hears the cells close to it, and the big ones best
  • read a compound action potential: the sum of many fibres, with waves for the Aα, Aβ, Aδ and C groups arriving at their own speeds, and growing as a stronger shock recruits more fibres
  • measure a conduction velocity from two latencies, and say why one latency is not enough
  • predict what a local anaesthetic and loss of myelin do to the recording

  • Switch between saline and brain tissue. The same current gives about four times the voltage in tissue: Ohm's law, V = IR, with a bigger R.
  • Turn on the noise and find the smallest axon whose spike you can still see. On Newt, Find action potentials sets its threshold as a number of noise SDs for the same reason.
  • The spike is computed once, when the page opens, in a cable of membrane patches with the cortical Na⁺ and K⁺ channels of Traub and Miles (as Pospischil and colleagues used them), sped up three times to body temperature, with cytoplasm of 35 Ω·cm. A wider axon gets the same spike shape, travelling faster (speed grows with the square root of the diameter).
  • The outside voltage adds up the membrane current of every bit of a long, straight axon, each weighted by 1/distance, in a fluid that is the same everywhere (the line-source model of Clark and Plonsey). Real tissue is not uniform, and a real electrode is not a point: a big electrode averages over its surface and records less.
  • A myelinated axon passes current only at its nodes, so the signal is lumpier along the axon and bigger near a node. Cell bodies and dendrites carry much more current than an axon; most spikes recorded in the brain come from cell bodies, at 50–500 µV.
  • This axon has no undershoot after its spike, so the third phase outside is small and long. Axons with an undershoot have a clearer third phase.

Every sweep that runs to the end adds a row. In the lab you would read these off the Record page; here they are collected for you. Choose what goes on each axis.

  • The picture: a mixed nerve, like the sciatic, laid across electrodes. S is the stimulating pair at the left (the shock leaves from the − electrode); R1 records, at the distance set, and R2 is the reference electrode beyond it. Each dot is one fibre that the shock fired, in rows by group: Aα at the top, then Aβ, Aδ, and the many small C fibres at the bottom. The dots move at their own conduction speeds, so they spread out as they go. Shown at the size of a real nerve chamber, 0 to 165 mm.
  • Monophasic: the nerve is crushed under R2, so spikes never reach it, and the recording is R1 alone: one hump per wave. Biphasic: the nerve is intact, each spike passes R1 and then R2, and the recording is their difference: up, then down. Set how far apart they are.
  • Charts: the recording at R1, against time after the shock. Up is R1 negative compared with R2, the usual way a CAP is shown. Thin coloured lines are each group's share; the black line is what an amplifier would record, their sum. The sharp blip at time 0 is the stimulus artefact: the shock itself, picked up directly through the fluid. Sizes are percentages of the full A wave at 40 mm. The faint grey line is the sweep before.
  • Shock strength is in multiples of the threshold of the biggest fibres. Local anaesthetic is applied to the middle of the nerve (the purple pool), as a percentage of the dose that blocks every fibre. Myelin damage slows every myelinated fibre by its own random amount; the worst-hit stop conducting.
  • Readouts: how many fibres fired, and when each group's wave peaks. In a real recording you would find each bump's top on the trace.
  • Raise the shock from below threshold in small steps and stimulate each time. Plot A wave size against Shock strength (log X): the CAP grows smoothly from nothing to a maximum as more fibres join, unlike the all-or-none spike of one fibre. In the lab this recruitment curve is one of the first things you measure.
  • Watch the dots. The fibres start together but arrive apart: a fast group as a tight bunch, a slow group spread out. That is why the slow waves are low and broad.
  • Record at several distances with a strong shock and plot each group's latency against Recording distance. Each group lies on a straight line whose slope is 1/speed. The lines don't go through zero: the wave takes time to rise to its peak.
  • The C fibres are the most numerous, yet their wave is tiny. Each thin fibre carries little current (it scales with the fibre's cross-section), and their slow, spread-out arrival smears the wave further.
  • Switch to biphasic and move R2 closer. When the electrodes are closer together than the length of axon a spike occupies, R2 sees the spike before R1 has finished, and the two halves cancel.
  • Add local anaesthetic slowly. The Aδ wave goes first, then the thinner Aβ fibres. The C wave holds out longer than you might guess for the thinnest fibres; the Theory explains why.
  • Damage the myelin: the A waves come later (slowing), spread out (each fibre is slowed by a different amount, called temporal dispersion) and shrink (conduction block in the worst fibres). The C wave doesn't change: C fibres have no myelin to lose.
  • The nerve has 740 fibres: 40 Aα, 80 Aβ, 120 Aδ and 500 C. A real nerve has thousands, with more C fibres than this. Their diameters are spread across each group's range.
  • Speeds follow Hursh's rule for myelinated fibres (about 6 m/s per µm of diameter) and 1.7 m/s × √(diameter in µm) for C fibres. Each fibre's contribution is one fixed hump, with a size that grows with its cross-section (smaller for C fibres) and a width set by its type.
  • Threshold is inversely proportional to diameter: big fibres are easiest to fire from outside. Real thresholds also depend on where each fibre lies in the nerve.
  • Every spike starts the instant the shock lands. Real fibres take a fraction of a millisecond to reach threshold (the utilization time), one more reason to measure speed from two distances.
  • The anaesthetic doses are illustrative. They follow the order found in recordings from single fibres: thin myelinated fibres first, C fibres among the last.
  • The temperature is fixed. Cold nerves, like a frog's at room temperature, conduct several times more slowly than a warm mammal's.

Theory

In plain words: The salty fluid around a cell is a resistor. Current that leaves the cell through its membrane has to flow through that fluid, and Ohm's law says a current through a resistor makes a voltage drop. An electrode outside measures that drop: big where current is crossing the membrane close by, tiny everywhere else.

Ohm's law in a bath

Suppose a current I leaves a tiny patch of membrane and spreads out evenly in all directions through fluid of resistivity ρ. Think of the fluid as a set of nested shells around the patch. The current has to cross every shell, and the thin shells close in are small, so they resist most; far out the shells are huge and hardly resist at all. Adding up the shells from a distance r out to far away gives a resistance R = ρ/(4πr), and Ohm's law gives the voltage at distance r, compared with far away:

\[ \phi = I\,R = \frac{\rho\, I}{4\pi r} \]

φ (phi) is the extracellular potential: what an electrode at distance r records against a far-away reference electrode. Current coming out of the cell makes the nearby fluid positive; current going in makes it negative.

A worked number

In this page's model, a spike on a 10 µm axon draws in about — nA of Na⁺ current, along the short stretch where it is rising. If all of it went in at one point 50 µm from an electrode in brain tissue (ρ = 300 Ω·cm = 3 Ω·m):

\[ R = \frac{3\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}}{4\pi \times 50\times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}} \approx 4.8\ \mathrm{k\Omega} \]

and Ohm's law gives φ = I R ≈ — µV.

The model gives — µV for the dip, smaller than this, for two reasons: the inward current is spread along about a millimetre of axon, much of it farther away than 50 µm, and the outward currents on either side make the fluid positive and cancel part of it. Either way: tens of nanoamps through a few kilohms makes a hundred or so microvolts, a few hundred times smaller than the 100 mV spike inside.

Why three phases

As a spike travels along an axon, three things happen at once along its length (The Action Potential Travels shows them on its currents chart):

  • Ahead of the spike current flows out through the membrane: it has come along the inside of the axon from the spike and is charging the next patch's capacitor, the charging of Passive Properties: Time.
  • At the spike Na⁺ flows in.
  • Behind the spike K⁺ flows out as the membrane repolarizes.

An electrode beside the axon feels each of these in turn as the spike goes by: outward current (positive), then inward (negative, the biggest), then outward again (positive). That is the triphasic extracellular spike. Its shape follows the membrane current next to the electrode, the dashed line on the Outside chart, not the voltage inside.

Show the derivation

The cable equation of Passive Properties: Space says that the current leaving the membrane per unit length of axon, im, is set by how sharply the voltage inside bends along the axon:

\[ i_\mathrm{m} = \frac{1}{r_\mathrm{i}}\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}, \qquad r_\mathrm{i} = \frac{4\rho_\mathrm{i}}{\pi d^2} \]

where ri is the resistance of the cytoplasm per unit length, for an axon of diameter d and cytoplasm resistivity ρi. (Current only leaves where the inside current changes, and by Ohm's law along the inside, the inside current is set by the slope of V.) For a spike travelling at speed θ without changing shape, V(x, t) = S(t − x/θ), where S(t) is the spike recorded at one place; so \( \partial^2 V/\partial x^2 = S''/\theta^2 \). Each bit of axon dx is a point source of current im dx, and the potentials of point sources add up:

\[ \phi(t) = \frac{\rho}{4\pi}\int \frac{i_\mathrm{m}(x,t)}{\sqrt{x^2 + h^2}}\,dx = \frac{\rho\, d^2}{16\,\rho_\mathrm{i}\,\theta^2}\int \frac{S''(t - x/\theta)}{\sqrt{x^2 + h^2}}\,dx \]

with h the electrode's distance from the axon. This is the sum the simulation does (the line-source model of Clark and Plonsey, 1968). Close to the axon, the 1/distance weighting is sharp and φ looks like S'', the second derivative of the spike in time: up as the voltage starts to curve upward, down at the steepest part of the rise, up again as the rise and fall slow. Farther away it blurs over a longer stretch of axon, and φ gets smaller and smoother.

In plain words: The spike's current goes round a loop: in through the membrane, along the inside, out through the membrane, and back through the fluid outside. The membrane resists far more than the fluid, so nearly all the voltage drop is across the membrane, and only a sliver is left in the fluid where an outside electrode can measure it. Move away and the sliver shrinks fast.

A voltage divider

Follow the current of one spike round its loop. The membrane is a big resistor (Passive Properties: Time); the cytoplasm inside is a smaller one; the fluid outside, with its huge cross-section, is the smallest of all. The same current flows through each part of a loop, so by Ohm's law each part takes a share of the voltage in proportion to its resistance. The fluid's share is tiny. That is why 100 mV inside becomes tens to hundreds of microvolts outside, and why outside recordings need amplifiers.

It also says how to get a bigger signal: make the outside path resist more. Brain tissue, with cells crowding the fluid into narrow gaps, has about four times the resistivity of saline, and gives four times the signal. In a nerve chamber, lifting the nerve out of the fluid into air or oil leaves only a thin film of fluid around it, and the CAP grows from microvolts to millivolts.

Falling off with distance

Measured in this model on a 10 µm axon in brain tissue, the size of the extracellular spike (peak to peak) is — µV at 50 µm, — µV at 300 µm and — µV at 1 mm. It falls in three stages:

  • Close to the axon (much closer than the length of axon the spike occupies, a millimetre or two here), the axon looks like a long line of current, and the voltage falls slowly, more slowly than 1/r.
  • Farther out the axon starts to look short, and the fall steepens toward 1/r, like a point source.
  • Far away, the inward and outward currents look as if they come from the same place. Every bit of current that goes in comes out again nearby, so their effects cancel, and what is left falls like 1/r² or faster.

This is why an electrode in the brain records the spikes of only the neurons within about 100 µm, and why it picks out big cells: they carry more current. The thinnest axons, like the C fibres of pain, make spikes of a few microvolts even close by, lost in the noise.

Show why the far field cancels

Seen from a distance R much larger than the stretch of axon carrying current, every 1/distance is nearly 1/R, so

\[ \phi \approx \frac{\rho}{4\pi R}\int i_\mathrm{m}\,dx + \cdots \]

But \( \int i_\mathrm{m}\,dx = 0 \): the axon doesn't store charge for long, so the current going in equals the current coming out. The 1/R term vanishes, leaving terms that fall as 1/R² (from the in and out currents being in slightly different places, a dipole) and 1/R³ (a quadrupole: out, in, out). For a spike that returns fully to rest the dipole term is zero too. Plot your own measurements on log scales: the slope of the line is the power of r.

In plain words: A nerve is a cable of thousands of axons. A shock fires some of them, and an electrode further along picks up the sum of all their small signals. Each axon's signal arrives after a delay set by its speed, so the sum comes in waves, fast fibres first. A stronger shock fires more fibres, so the sum grows: it is graded, not all or none.

Many fibres, added up

Record from the outside of a whole nerve and each fibre that fires adds its own small extracellular signal. The fibre's signal arrives after a latency L/θ, its distance divided by its speed. The sum is the compound action potential (CAP). Erlanger and Gasser recorded it with the new cathode-ray oscilloscope in the 1920s and saw that it comes in separate waves, one for each group of fibre sizes:

GroupDiameterMyelinSpeedCarries

C0.2–1.5 µmno0.5–2 m/sdull, burning pain, warmth, itch

Graded by the shock

A single fibre is all or none, but the CAP grows step by step with the shock, because a stronger shock reaches the threshold of more fibres. Big fibres have the lowest threshold to a shock from outside: the shock's current flows along the nerve, and a wide fibre, with its low internal resistance, takes in more of it. So the Aα wave appears first, then Aβ and Aδ, and the C wave needs a shock tens of times stronger. Once every fibre fires, a stronger shock adds nothing: the CAP is maximal.

Why the slow waves are small

A thin fibre carries little current, and the size of its signal outside grows with its cross-section (d²). And the further the waves travel, the more the fibres in a group, each with a slightly different speed, drift apart: a slow wave is smeared over a long time, so it is low and broad. This temporal dispersion grows with distance.

Monophasic and biphasic

With two recording electrodes on an intact nerve, each spike passes under R1 and then R2, and the amplifier records their difference: a hump one way and then the other, biphasic. Crushing or numbing the nerve under R2 stops the spikes there and leaves only the first hump: monophasic, easier to read. If the electrodes are closer than the length of nerve a spike occupies (its speed × its duration, several centimetres for a warm Aα fibre), the two humps overlap and partly cancel.

In plain words: Record the same wave at two distances. The extra distance divided by the extra time is the speed. One distance and one latency won't do, because the latency includes delays that have nothing to do with travelling: the time for the shock to start the spike, and for the wave to rise to the point you measure.

Two latencies

\[ \theta = \frac{L_2 - L_1}{t_2 - t_1} \]

In this model, with a strong shock, the Aα wave peaks at — ms when recorded 40 mm from the stimulating electrode and at — ms at 80 mm. The speed is 40 mm ÷ — ms = — m/s (1 mm/ms is 1 m/s). Dividing 40 mm by the first latency alone would give — m/s, far too slow: about — ms of that latency is the time the wave takes to rise to its peak, which is the same at both distances and cancels when you subtract.

Any fixed point of the wave works, as long as it is the same at both distances: the start (onset) of the wave gives the speed of the fastest fibres in the group; the peak gives the speed of the most common ones. Clinical labs use the onset.

From speed to size

For myelinated fibres the speed is about 6 m/s for each µm of diameter (Hursh, 1939), so an Aα wave at 96 m/s comes from fibres about 16 µm wide. For bare fibres speed grows only with the square root of diameter (The Action Potential Travels explains why).

Show it as a straight line

If the wave travels at θ after a fixed delay t0, its latency at distance L is

\[ t = t_0 + \frac{L}{\theta} \]

a straight line against L with slope 1/θ and intercept t0. Two points fix the line; subtracting them removes t0. With more distances you can check that the points really lie on a line, which is what Your measurements will show if you plot a group's latency against Recording distance. In plain words: Local anaesthetics block Na⁺ channels, so spikes stop where the drug is. Thin myelinated fibres stop first, so sharp pain and cold go before touch and movement. Diseases that strip myelin slow the big fibres unevenly: their waves come late, spread out and small.

Local anaesthetics

Lidocaine and its relatives get into the axon and plug its Na⁺ channels from the inside. Without the inward Na⁺ current there is nothing to regenerate the spike, which fades out by passive spread, as with TTX in The Action Potential Travels.

Which fibres go first? The textbook rule is that thin fibres are blocked first. Recordings from single fibres agree for myelinated fibres: Aδ before Aβ before Aα. A spike can jump over one or two blocked nodes of Ranvier, so it only stops where about three nodes in a row are blocked. Thin fibres have shorter internodes, so more of their nodes lie in the drug's path (Franz and Perry, 1974). But bare C fibres, the thinnest of all, turn out to be among the hardest to block (Gokin and colleagues, 2001). In the clinic cold and pinprick still go first, touch next and movement last, and that is the order the simulation shows.

Demyelination and axon loss

In demyelinating neuropathies, such as the commonest form of Guillain–Barré syndrome and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP), the immune system strips myelin in patches. A damaged internode leaks current and charges slowly, so the spike slows, and where too much myelin is lost it stops (conduction block). Each fibre is hit differently, so a group's spikes arrive spread out (temporal dispersion). The CAP is late, wide and small.

In axonal neuropathies the axons themselves die. The fibres that are left conduct at normal speed, so the waves come on time but are smaller. Nerve conduction studies tell the two apart by measuring both latency and size.

Recording a CAP

  • Each stimulus is a trial. With the stimulator docked under Trials on the Record page, each shock starts a sweep. Make each trial long enough for the waves you want: a few ms for the A waves of a warm nerve, longer for a cold one or for C fibres.
  • Time zero is the artefact. The sharp blip at the start is the shock itself, picked up through the fluid. Measure each wave's latency from it. A bigger shock makes a bigger artefact; moving the recording electrodes farther from the stimulating ones makes it smaller.
  • Sample fast enough. An A wave can be over in half a millisecond. To see its shape you need a sample every 20–50 µs, a sampling rate of 20–50 kHz.
  • Find threshold, then the maximum. Start with the shock too weak to give anything, and raise it in small steps. Note where a wave first appears and where it stops growing. Plotting the CAP's size against shock strength gives the recruitment curve, as in Your measurements above.
  • Get a speed from two distances. Move the recording electrodes (or the stimulating ones) a measured distance along the nerve, record again, and use the calculator below. Measure the distances along the nerve, from the stimulating − electrode to the first recording electrode.
  • Check it is the nerve. A real CAP moves later when the recording electrodes move away from the stimulating ones; the artefact doesn't. Crushing the nerve between them abolishes the CAP but not the artefact.

Recording spikes from outside

  • Spikes recorded from outside are small (tens to hundreds of µV) and brief, so set the input range and amplifier gain to suit, and watch the noise.
  • Find action potentials detects spikes when the trace crosses a threshold set as a number of noise SDs, and can require each spike to swing both ways, as real extracellular spikes do (artifacts often don't).
  • Waveforms shows the shape of the spikes found. With an extracellular recording this is the shape of the current crossing the membrane next to the electrode, not the membrane voltage, as this lesson's first part shows. Spikes of different shapes and sizes usually come from different cells, at different distances from the electrode.
