Slow signals: metabotropic receptors and neuromodulation

The receptors in the Chemical Synapse lesson are channels: transmitter binds and the pore opens within a millisecond. Many receptors aren’t channels at all. Transmitter binding switches on a G protein inside the cell, and only at the end of a short chain of proteins does a channel open or close, tens to hundreds of milliseconds later. These metabotropic receptors make slow synaptic potentials, and they are how dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline and acetylcholine change the way a neuron answers everything else it hears.

You'll be able to
  • contrast an ionotropic receptor (a channel: fast and brief) with a metabotropic one (a G-protein-coupled receptor: slow, long-lasting and amplified)
  • follow a slow IPSP step by step: GABAB receptor → G protein → βγ subunits → GIRK K⁺ channel → V pulled toward EK, and work out its current with Ohm’s law
  • explain why a single spike gives almost no GABAB response but a burst gives a large one
  • explain neuromodulation: acetylcholine closing M-type K⁺ channels makes the same input give more spikes, without slowing down
  • name drugs that act on G-protein-coupled receptors: baclofen, opioids, atropine, β-blockers, antipsychotics

Theory

In plain words: An ionotropic receptor is a channel with a transmitter switch: fast and brief, one receptor opening one pore. A metabotropic receptor is a switch with no pore. It turns on G proteins inside the cell, and they do the work: open or close channels, or start enzymes that make messengers like cAMP. It is slower and lasts longer, and it amplifies, because one receptor can switch on many G proteins.

The G-protein switch

Every metabotropic receptor is one protein that crosses the membrane seven times. Its partner inside the cell is a G protein made of three parts, α, β and γ (β and γ stay together as βγ). The switch works in four steps:

  1. Transmitter binds the receptor on the outside of the cell.
  2. The receptor makes the α subunit let go of the GDP it holds and pick up GTP instead. That is the “on” switch.
  3. α·GTP and βγ come apart, and each can act on its own targets: channels, or enzymes.
  4. α slowly splits its GTP into GDP (it is an enzyme, a GTPase, with its own timer), joins βγ again, and the signal stops.

Step 4 sets how long the response lasts: not how long transmitter stays around, but how long α keeps its GTP, hundreds of milliseconds or more. The receptor can switch on G protein after G protein while transmitter is bound, which is where amplification starts.

Three families of G protein

  • Gi/o (“inhibitory”): its βγ opens GIRK K⁺ channels and closes voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; its α lowers cAMP. GABAB, μ-opioid, dopamine D2, M2 muscarinic.
  • Gs (“stimulatory”): its α turns on adenylyl cyclase, which makes cAMP. β-adrenergic (noradrenaline), dopamine D1.
  • Gq: its α turns on phospholipase C (PLC), which splits the membrane lipid PIP2 into two messengers, IP3 (releases Ca²⁺ from stores) and DAG. M1 muscarinic, mGluR1, α1-adrenergic.

In plain words: GABA on a GABAB receptor switches on G proteins. Their βγ parts open GIRK K⁺ channels, and K⁺ leaving the cell pulls V down toward EK. It starts late, because each step takes time, and it lasts as long as the G proteins stay on. A GIRK channel needs four βγ to open, so it takes a burst of spikes, not one.

Ohm’s law with the K⁺ battery

Once open, a GIRK channel is a K⁺ conductance like any other (Membrane Potential lesson). The current through all of them is the conductance times the driving force:

\[ I_\mathrm{GIRK} = g_\mathrm{GIRK}(t)\,\bigl(V - E_\mathrm{K}\bigr) \]

EK is about −90 mV, below rest, so at any normal V the current is outward and pulls V down: a hyperpolarizing IPSP. For example, with gGIRK = 0.17 mS/cm² and the cell at −60 mV, I = 0.17 × (−60 − (−90)) = 0.17 × 30 ≈ 5.1 µA/cm², outward. The GABAA IPSP uses a different battery, ECl, about −75 mV in this cell.

How far V goes

With the GIRK channels open, the cell has two conductances pulling it to two batteries, its resting conductance toward Erest and GIRK toward EK, and V settles at their conductance-weighted average, the chord-conductance formula from the Membrane Potential lesson:

\[ V = \frac{g_\mathrm{rest}E_\mathrm{rest} + g_\mathrm{GIRK}E_\mathrm{K}}{g_\mathrm{rest} + g_\mathrm{GIRK}} \]

With grest = 0.1 mS/cm² at −70 mV and gGIRK = 0.17 mS/cm²: V = (0.1 × −70 + 0.17 × −90) / 0.27 ≈ −83 mV, a 13 mV IPSP if it lasted long enough to settle. Try other numbers in the calculator below.

Why a burst, not a spike

A GIRK channel is made of four subunits, and each can hold one βγ. The channel opens only when all four sites are filled, so the fraction of channels open depends on the fourth power of the active G protein, G:

\[ \text{fraction of GIRK open} = \frac{G^4}{G^4 + K_\mathrm{d}} \]

In this simulation Kd = 100, after Destexhe and Sejnowski, with G measured in units of what one presynaptic spike switches on. One spike: G = 1, so 1 / 101 = 1% of channels open, an IPSP of well under a millivolt. A burst of five spikes piles up about five times as much G protein: 5⁴ = 625, and 625 / 725 = 86% open. Five times the G protein, eighty-six times the channels. It is the same trick as the fourth power of Ca²⁺ in transmitter release (Chemical Synapse lesson): a threshold built from molecules cooperating.

Show the derivation

Suppose a closed channel C becomes an open channel O only when it has bound four βγ, and that the in-between states are short-lived, so we can ignore them:

\[ \mathrm{C} + 4\,\beta\gamma \;\rightleftharpoons\; \mathrm{O}, \qquad K_\mathrm{d} = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]\,[\beta\gamma]^4}{[\mathrm{O}]} \]

Kd is the dissociation constant: at equilibrium, the product of what has come apart over what is together. Solve it for [C] = Kd[O] / [βγ]⁴ and put that into the fraction of channels open:

\[ \frac{[\mathrm{O}]}{[\mathrm{C}] + [\mathrm{O}]} = \frac{[\mathrm{O}]}{K_\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{O}]/[\beta\gamma]^4 + [\mathrm{O}]} = \frac{[\beta\gamma]^4}{K_\mathrm{d} + [\beta\gamma]^4} \]

This is a Hill equation with Hill coefficient 4. Half the channels are open when [βγ]⁴ = Kd, that is at [βγ] = Kd1/4 = 1001/4 ≈ 3.2 spikes’ worth. Well below that, doubling G multiplies the open fraction by 2⁴ = 16.

Timing

The GABAA conductance follows the transmitter: it rises in a millisecond and is gone in tens. The GIRK conductance waits for every step of the chain: G proteins switching on, βγ reaching the channels, four of them binding. It starts tens of milliseconds after the burst, peaks after about 100–200 ms, and lasts until α has turned its GTP back into GDP, several hundred milliseconds. In the brain a second effect adds to the need for a burst: GABAB receptors sit around the edges of the synapse, and GABA reaches them in quantity only when many vesicles are released together and it spills out.

In plain words: A neuromodulator often barely moves the resting voltage. What it changes is how the cell answers its other inputs: more spikes or fewer, slowing down or not. Acetylcholine does it by shutting K⁺ channels that would otherwise build up and brake the firing.

The M current, the brake on firing

Pyramidal cells have slow voltage-gated K⁺ channels called M channels (KV7, made of KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 subunits). They open slowly when the cell depolarizes and close slowly again, over hundreds of milliseconds. Each spike opens a few more, so during a long input the outward K⁺ current grows and the firing slows: adaptation (Firing Rate lesson). Their current is Ohm’s law once more:

\[ I_\mathrm{M} = g_\mathrm{M}\,p_\mathrm{M}(t)\,\bigl(V - E_\mathrm{K}\bigr) \]

where pM is the fraction of M channels open. At rest, −70 mV, pM is about 3%, so the M channels hardly matter. While the cell fires they open further, and late in Part 2’s step they are about half open. With gM = 0.1 mS/cm², between spikes at about −53 mV, the M current is then 0.1 × 0.5 × (−53 − (−90)) ≈ 1.9 µA/cm², outward: nearly half of a 4 µA/cm² step goes into cancelling it.

How acetylcholine closes them

Acetylcholine on M1 muscarinic receptors switches on Gq, whose α turns on phospholipase C. PLC cuts up PIP2, a lipid in the inner face of the membrane, and M channels can’t open without PIP2. So the more acetylcholine, the fewer M channels left to open; the “M” stands for muscarinic, because this is how the current was found. With no brake building up, the same input gives more spikes and the rate stays up.

Show why closing K⁺ channels also makes the cell easier to excite

For a small, steady input current I, the voltage change is Ohm’s law with the cell’s total conductance:

\[ \Delta V = \frac{I}{g_\mathrm{total}} \]

Close some K⁺ channels and gtotal falls, so the same input moves V further. If K⁺ channels made up 0.15 of a total 0.25 mS/cm², closing all of them would leave 0.1: a 1 µA/cm² input would move V by 10 mV instead of 4 mV. Closing open K⁺ channels also depolarizes the cell a little, since less K⁺ leaves; in this simulation that is small, because at rest the M channels are nearly all shut already.

Other modulators, the same idea

Noradrenaline on β receptors switches on Gs, which raises cAMP; cAMP turns on protein kinase A, which shuts the Ca²⁺-activated K⁺ channels behind the slow afterhyperpolarization, another brake on firing. The cell stops adapting, as with acetylcholine. Acetylcholine comes from a few nuclei in the basal forebrain and noradrenaline from the locus coeruleus, and each sends axons all over the cortex, releasing its modulator from swellings along the way rather than at one tight synapse. That is part of how waking, attention and arousal change the way the whole cortex responds.

In plain words: Each step of a G-protein chain can multiply the signal: one receptor switches on many G proteins, one enzyme makes many messenger molecules, one kinase changes many proteins. That is how a few molecules of a hormone or modulator can have a large effect, and why the effect can outlast the transmitter by seconds to hours.

Multiplying at each step

A receptor with transmitter bound switches on one G protein after another until the transmitter leaves. If the G protein’s target is an enzyme, such as adenylyl cyclase, each enzyme makes many cAMP molecules, and each protein kinase that cAMP turns on adds phosphate to many proteins. As an example with made-up but reasonable numbers: 10 G proteins per receptor, each turning on a cyclase that makes 100 cAMP, gives 1000 cAMP from one receptor, before the kinase step multiplies again. In the eye, one rhodopsin molecule hit by light switches on many G proteins in the same way, which is how a rod can respond to a single photon.

Two speeds of metabotropic signalling

  • Direct, through the membrane: βγ reaches a channel near the receptor, as in Part 1. No messenger leaves the membrane. Tens to hundreds of milliseconds, and local.
  • Through a second messenger: cAMP, IP3, Ca²⁺ or DAG spreads through the cell and turns on kinases. Seconds to minutes; it can change many kinds of channel at once, and kinases that reach the nucleus can change which genes are read, the start of changes that last for days.

The slow end of this scale overlaps with learning: the long-lasting forms of the synaptic plasticity in the Spines and Plasticity lesson need kinases and new proteins.

In plain words: About a third of all prescription drugs act on G-protein-coupled receptors. Some switch a receptor on (agonists), more of them block it (antagonists). Knowing the chain, receptor → G protein → target, tells you what a drug will do and what its side effects will be.

When the M channels are wrong

Mutations in KCNQ2, one of the two M-channel genes, cause seizures in newborn babies: with too little M current, neurons fire too easily. Retigabine (ezogabine), a drug that holds M channels open, was approved for epilepsy and later withdrawn because of its side effects.
