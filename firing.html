Firing rate and firing patterns: how a neuron says “how much”

In the Hodgkin–Huxley lesson one brief pulse fired one spike. Hold the current on and a neuron fires again and again. Every spike is the same size, so a neuron can’t report a bigger input with a bigger spike. It reports it with more spikes per second. This lesson is about that rate: what sets it, what caps it, why some cells slow down while the input is still on, and why others fire in bursts.

You'll be able to
  • explain why a steady current gives a train of spikes: after each spike the membrane recharges like an RC circuit until it reaches threshold again
  • build and read an f–I curve: the threshold current (rheobase), the rise in rate with current, and the ceiling set by the refractory period
  • explain adaptation: a slow K⁺ current (the M current) that builds up spike by spike and stretches the intervals
  • tell a regular-spiking cell (pyramidal, adapts) from a fast-spiking one (interneuron, doesn’t) and a bursting one (T-type Ca²⁺ channels)
  • predict what drugs that act on these channels do to firing: muscarinic agonists, retigabine, ethosuximide, phenytoin

Theory

In plain words: After a spike the voltage is back below threshold. The current you are still putting in charges the membrane back up, the same charging as the RC circuit in Passive Properties: Time. When it reaches threshold, the next spike fires, and the cycle starts again. The time that recharging takes is the gap between spikes. More current charges faster, so the gaps shrink and the rate goes up.

Rate, not size

Past threshold every spike is the same full size (the Hodgkin–Huxley lesson’s all-or-none). So a neuron can’t say “a stronger input” with a bigger spike. What it can change is how many spikes it fires per second: its firing rate.

Recharging, by Ohm’s law

Between spikes, the Na⁺ and fast K⁺ channels are mostly shut, and the membrane is a resistor (the leak, R = 1/gL) and a capacitor (C), with your current I flowing in. The current pushes V toward a new level, Ohm’s law’s V = I·R above rest:

\[ V_\infty = V_\text{rest} + I R = V_\text{rest} + \frac{I}{g_L} \]

and V climbs toward it along the same curve as the capacitor in Passive Properties: Time, fast at first and then more slowly, with time constant τ = RC = C/gL. If V∞ is above threshold, V gets there and fires; after the spike it starts again from below. If V∞ is below threshold, V just settles there and the cell stays silent.

A worked number: the threshold current

So the smallest current that keeps a cell firing, its rheobase, is the one that just lifts V∞ to threshold: the voltage gap divided by R. In the regular-spiking cell, gL = 0.1 mS/cm² (R = 10 kΩ·cm²), rest is −70 mV and steady firing needs V to reach about −50 mV, 20 mV higher:

\[ I_\text{rh} \approx (V_\text{th} - V_\text{rest})\,g_L = 20\ \text{mV} \times 0.1\ \text{mS/cm}^2 = 2.0\ \mu\text{A/cm}^2 \]

The simulation needs about 2.3 µA/cm². The difference: near threshold some M channels (a slow K⁺ channel, see Adaptation) are open too, adding their conductance to the leak’s, so it takes a little more current to hold V up there. The time constant is τ = C/gL = 1 µF/cm² ÷ 0.1 mS/cm² = 10 ms, so each recharge takes a few milliseconds at large currents and tens of milliseconds near rheobase.

Show the derivation (a leaky integrate-and-fire cell)

Keep only the RC circuit and a rule: when V reaches Vth, count a spike, hold V at Vreset for a refractory time tref, then let it charge again. With a steady current I, between spikes

\[ V(t) = V_\infty + (V_\text{reset} - V_\infty)\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad V_\infty = V_\text{rest} + \frac{I}{g_L}, \qquad \tau = \frac{C}{g_L} \]

The interval T is the time to climb from Vreset to Vth. Set V(T) = Vth and solve for T:

\[ e^{-T/\tau} = \frac{V_\infty - V_\text{th}}{V_\infty - V_\text{reset}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad T = \tau \ln\frac{V_\infty - V_\text{reset}}{V_\infty - V_\text{th}}, \qquad f = \frac{1}{t_\text{ref} + T} \]

When V∞ is only just above Vth, the bottom of the fraction is tiny, the logarithm is large, and the cell fires slowly. As I grows, the fraction approaches 1, the logarithm approaches 0, and f approaches 1/tref. The integrate-and-fire calculator at the bottom of the page works this out for any numbers you choose.

The integrate-and-fire cell: change the current and see the gap and the rate.

In plain words: Run steps of different sizes and plot each one’s rate against its current: that is the cell’s f–I curve. Below the rheobase it is flat at zero. Above it the rate climbs, steeply at first, then more slowly, and it flattens out at a ceiling of a few hundred spikes per second. The slope tells you how many extra spikes per second each extra bit of current buys.

Reading the curve

  • Where it leaves zero: the rheobase, the smallest current that keeps the cell firing. A cell with more leak (bigger gL, smaller R) needs more current: Ohm’s law again.
  • The slope: the cell’s gain. In the regular-spiking cell the rate from the first interval goes from about 91 spikes/s at 4 µA/cm² to about 139 at 6: some 24 spikes/s for each extra µA/cm².
  • The top: the ceiling set by the refractory period (next tab). Near it, more current buys almost nothing.

First interval and last interval

A cell that adapts has two f–I curves. The first interval, at the start of a step, gives the higher one; the last interval, after the slow K⁺ current has built up, the lower one. In a fast-spiking cell the two are the same. The gap between them is a measure of how much the cell adapts.

Two ways to start firing

In 1948 Hodgkin stepped the current into single crab nerve fibres and found that they started firing in different ways. Some could fire very slowly just above threshold, with the rate rising smoothly from almost zero, like this page’s regular-spiking cell (2.4 µA/cm² gives gaps of some 30 ms and longer). Others jumped straight to a rate of tens of spikes per second, or fired only once however long the step. The squid axon of the Hodgkin–Huxley lesson is of that second kind. Which kind a cell is depends on the mix of its channels.

Show the derivation: why the curve bends

From the integrate-and-fire interval (Spike after spike), with V∞ = Vrest + I/gL:

\[ f = \frac{1}{t_\text{ref} + \tau \ln\dfrac{V_\infty - V_\text{reset}}{V_\infty - V_\text{th}}} \]

Just above rheobase the logarithm blows up, so f rises from 0 very steeply. For large I the fraction is close to 1, and ln(1 + x) ≈ x gives

\[ T \approx \tau\,\frac{V_\text{th} - V_\text{reset}}{V_\infty - V_\text{th}} \approx \frac{C\,(V_\text{th} - V_\text{reset})}{I} \]

which is just the capacitor’s rule from Passive Properties: Time: charging C by ΔV = Vth − Vreset with a current I takes C·ΔV/I. Then the rate rises in a straight line, f ≈ I/(C·ΔV): with C = 1 µF/cm² and ΔV = 15 mV, about 67 spikes/s per µA/cm². Last, tref takes over and bends the line flat, toward 1/tref.

In plain words: Right after a spike the cell can’t fire again straight away: its Na⁺ channels are still plugged by inactivation and its K⁺ channels are still open. It takes a few milliseconds to recover. However hard you push, the spikes can’t come closer than that, so the rate tops out at a few hundred spikes per second.

The refractory period

As in the Hodgkin–Huxley lesson: during a spike the Na⁺ channels’ h gates close (inactivation) and the K⁺ channels’ n gates open. Until h has reopened and n has closed again, fewer Na⁺ channels are available and extra K⁺ current flows out. For about a millisecond no current can fire another spike (the absolute refractory period); for a few milliseconds more only a larger current can (the relative refractory period).

A worked number: in the regular-spiking cell at 25 µA/cm² the spikes come about 3.1 ms apart, 1 ÷ 3.1 ms ≈ 320 spikes/s. Ten times the threshold current, and the rate has nearly stopped rising.

Why the spikes shrink

At the highest rates each spike starts before the last one’s h gates have fully reopened. With fewer Na⁺ channels available, less Na⁺ comes in, and the spike peaks lower. In the simulation the peaks reach about +48 mV at 6 µA/cm², but only about +31 mV once a train at 25 µA/cm² is under way.

A drug that works here

Phenytoin and carbamazepine, two common drugs for epilepsy, bind most tightly to Na⁺ channels that are inactivated, and slow their recovery. A cell firing slowly has time to recover between spikes and barely notices. A cell firing in a fast, seizure-like barrage gets no such time, so its channels pile up in the blocked state. The drugs cut the highest firing rates much more than normal ones (they are called use-dependent).

Show why no current can pass the ceiling

Each cycle is a refractory time plus a recharge time, and the recharge can shrink toward zero but never below it:

\[ f = \frac{1}{t_\text{ref} + T} \;<\; \frac{1}{t_\text{ref}} \]

With tref = 3 ms the ceiling is 1/0.003 s ≈ 330 spikes/s. In a real cell (and in the model) tref isn’t a fixed number: a bigger current can fire the cell earlier in the relative refractory period, which is why the rate keeps creeping up a little.

In plain words: Some K⁺ channels open so slowly that one spike barely moves them, but each spike opens a few more, and between spikes they close only slowly. Over a train of spikes they build up. The K⁺ current they let out works against your current, so each recharge takes longer and the cell slows down, though the input hasn’t changed. That is adaptation.

The M current

In the model the slow channels are M channels, made of KV7 (KCNQ2 and KCNQ3) proteins. They open with depolarization, over tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Their current is Ohm’s law with a battery, like every channel since the Membrane Potential lesson:

\[ I_M = \bar g_M\, p\,(V - E_K) \]

where p, the fraction open, creeps up with every spike. Watch the M bar in the circuit during a long step: it starts near 0.03 and climbs.

A worked number

In the regular-spiking cell at 4 µA/cm², by the end of a 1.2 s step about 60% of the M channels are open (p ≈ 0.6). Over the train V averages about −50 mV, 40 mV above EK:

\[ I_M \approx 0.07\ \text{mS/cm}^2 \times 0.6 \times 40\ \text{mV} \approx 1.7\ \mu\text{A/cm}^2 \ \text{(outward)} \]

So of the 4 µA/cm² you put in, nearly half now leaks straight back out through M channels instead of charging the membrane. The recharge is slower, the gaps longer: the rate falls from about 91 to about 49 spikes/s.

Switching it off

Acetylcholine, acting through muscarinic receptors and a G protein, closes M channels: that is how they were found, and why they are called M. With them closed, a pyramidal cell keeps firing at its first rate for as long as the input lasts. It is one of the clearest examples of a neuromodulator changing what a neuron does with the same input (more in Slow Signals: Metabotropic Receptors and Neuromodulation). Ca²⁺-activated K⁺ channels slow many cells in a similar way: Ca²⁺ enters with each spike and builds up, opening them.

When it goes wrong, and a drug

Mutations that weaken KCNQ2 or KCNQ3 channels cause an inherited epilepsy of newborns (benign familial neonatal seizures): too little M current, too little brake on firing. Retigabine (ezogabine) does the opposite: it makes M channels open at lower voltages, about 20 mV lower, so more are open at rest and during firing. It was approved for epilepsy and later withdrawn because of side effects, but it shows the idea: more M current, fewer spikes for the same input.

Show the derivation: how the M current builds up

The M gate follows the voltage slowly:

\[ \frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{p_\infty(V) - p}{\tau_p(V)} \]

During a spike V is high, p∞ is near 1, and p rises a little: say each spike opens a fraction δ of the channels still shut, p → p + δ(1 − p). Between spikes V is low, and p decays back with τp, hundreds of milliseconds. If spikes come every T milliseconds and T is much shorter than τp, the gain per spike balances the loss between spikes when

\[ \delta\,(1 - p) \approx p\,\frac{T}{\tau_p} \quad\Rightarrow\quad p \approx \frac{f\,\delta\,\tau_p}{1 + f\,\delta\,\tau_p}, \qquad f = \frac{1}{T} \]

So the faster the cell fires, the more M channels end up open, and the more it is slowed: adaptation pushes back hardest on high rates. Because τp is long, the build-up takes many spikes, which is why the slowing is gradual.

In plain words: Some cells fire in groups of spikes: bursts. In the bursting cell here, T-type Ca²⁺ channels open at voltages just above rest and let Ca²⁺ in. That gives a slow hump of depolarization, and a few fast Na⁺ spikes ride on top of it. But T channels inactivate if the cell sits depolarized, and only recover when it is held more negative. So the same cell bursts when it rests low and fires single spikes when it rests high.

The T channel

Its current is Ohm’s law with a Ca²⁺ battery, ECa ≈ +120 mV:

\[ I_T = \bar g_T\, m^2 h\,(V - E_\mathrm{Ca}) \]

It works like the Na⁺ channel of the Hodgkin–Huxley lesson, with a fast activation gate (m) and a slower inactivation gate (h), but at lower voltages, and more slowly. The m gates open from about −70 mV up. The h gates are open only below about −75 mV, and take tens of milliseconds to move. At −84 mV about 56% of the T channels are ready to open; at −67 mV, fewer than 2%.

Two modes

Thalamic relay cells, which pass sensory signals on to the cortex, are the classic example. When you are awake they sit depolarized, their T channels are inactivated, and they fire single spikes at a rate that follows their input: tonic mode, a faithful relay. In drowsiness and sleep they are held more negative, their T channels recover, and an input sets off a burst instead: burst mode. Inhibition can do the same: a pause of inhibition lets the T channels recover, and when it ends the cell fires a rebound burst.

Absence seizures

In absence epilepsy (“petit mal”), common in children, the thalamus and cortex lock into rhythmic bursting about three times a second, and the child briefly stops responding. Ethosuximide, a first choice for these seizures, reduces the T-type Ca²⁺ current in thalamic cells.

Show the T channel’s gates

The m gates follow V almost at once; the h gates relax with a time constant τh(V), tens of milliseconds at body temperature. With V in mV (Huguenard and McCormick’s fits, 2 mV more negative, as in the model this page uses):

\[ m_\infty = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(V + 59)/6.2}}, \qquad h_\infty = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(V + 83)/4}}, \qquad \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{h_\infty(V) - h}{\tau_h(V)} \]

At −84 mV, h∞ = 1/(1 + e−0.25) ≈ 0.56; at −67 mV, h∞ = 1/(1 + e4) ≈ 0.02. The channel conducts only when both are open, m²h, so it opens in a window: V must first sit low (to open h) and then rise (to open m). A burst closes its own window: the hump of depolarization closes h again, and the cell needs another spell at low voltage before it can burst again.

In plain words: The firing rate is how much of the nervous system reports “how much”: how strong a touch is, how hard a muscle should pull. The shape of a cell’s f–I curve, and whether and how it adapts or bursts, decides what it does with its input. Many drugs work by changing exactly these channels.

Rate codes

A sensory neuron fires faster for a stronger stimulus (Sensory Receptors: from Stimulus to Spikes). A motor neuron fires faster to make its muscle fibres pull harder (The Neuromuscular Junction). In each case the message is in the rate, read off by the next cell, which adds up the synaptic potentials the spikes cause (Summation and the Spike Trigger Zone).

Different cells for different jobs

Adaptation lets a cell report change rather than a steady state: it fires hard when its input starts and then calms down. Fast-spiking interneurons do the opposite job. They have little or no slow K⁺ current, and fast KV3 channels that end each spike quickly, so they can fire hundreds of narrow spikes per second without slowing, and keep inhibition in step with excitation. Bursting cells such as thalamic relay cells switch between passing their input on faithfully and signalling with bursts.

  • Voltage over time: the current is on from the start. V climbs from Vreset toward V∞ (dotted) like a charging capacitor; where it meets Vth (teal dashed) a spike is counted (drawn as a thin line up), and V is held at Vreset for tref before it charges again. If V∞ is below Vth, V just settles there.
  • f–I curve: the rate at every current from 0 to 25 µA/cm², worked out with the formula. Dashed: the ceiling, 1/tref. Dotted: the rheobase. The dot is the current set above.
  • The simulated cells above have no fixed threshold or reset: their Na⁺ and K⁺ channels make those. That is why the calculator’s numbers come out a little different from theirs.
