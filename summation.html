Adding up inputs: summation and where the spike starts

One synapse almost never makes a neuron fire. A neuron adds up hundreds of small inputs, and only if the total, at one special place at the start of the axon, reaches threshold do its voltage-gated Na⁺ channels (lesson 3) take over and fire a spike. See why inputs add on the membrane's capacitor, then follow them through a whole neuron from the dendrite to the axon initial segment.

You'll be able to
  • predict how the resting conductances and the capacitance set how large one input is and how long it lasts (lesson 2's τ = C / Σg)
  • explain temporal summation (inputs close in time ride on each other because the membrane has not yet discharged) and spatial summation (inputs on different synapses charge the same membrane)
  • explain why an input far out on a dendrite arrives smaller, and how inhibition near the cell body shunts the sum
  • explain why the spike starts in the axon initial segment, not the cell body: it has the most Na⁺ channels, so it reaches threshold first

Simulation

Start here
  1. On the capacitor, two inputs close together sum; two far apart do not.
  2. More K⁺ leak: each input is smaller and gone sooner, so less adds up.
  3. Fire 25 synapses on the dendrite (20 are not enough) and watch the voltage spread to the cell body and axon: the spike starts in the axon initial segment.
  4. The same synapses at 330 µm: too little reaches the cell body.
  5. With no Na⁺ channels in the axon initial segment, the same sum goes nowhere.

The marks along its two faces are the charge Q on the capacitor, one mark per 2 nC/cm². At rest the inside face carries negative charge and the outside positive: that is the resting voltage. Each kind of channel is a resistor with a battery

From lessons 1 and 2: Ohm's law says current is conductance times voltage, and an ion channel's voltage is the driving force, how far the membrane voltage V is from that ion's Nernst potential E:

\[ I_\mathrm{K} = g_\mathrm{K}\,(V - E_\mathrm{K}), \quad I_\mathrm{Na} = g_\mathrm{Na}\,(V - E_\mathrm{Na}), \quad I_\mathrm{Cl} = g_\mathrm{Cl}\,(V - E_\mathrm{Cl}) \]

At rest the three channel types are open a little, and each pulls the voltage toward its own battery. The voltage settles where the currents cancel, and that is a conductance-weighted average of the batteries:

\[ V_\mathrm{rest} = \frac{g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}E_\mathrm{Cl}}{g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}} \]

So whichever channel is open most wins the tug of war. Mostly K⁺ leak channels: rest near EK (about −86 mV). Add some Na⁺ leak: rest moves up, toward +60 mV. Cl⁻ sits at about −82 mV in a mature neuron (which pumps Cl⁻ out), close to rest, so it mostly anchors the voltage in place.

Why this matters for firing

Two things about the resting conductances decide whether inputs can fire the cell. First, where rest is: the farther Vrest is below the threshold, the more depolarization a neuron needs. Second, how big the total conductance is: a leaky membrane (big Σg) lets input current escape, so each input changes the voltage less and for a shorter time. Both are set by the same sliders.

\[ R_\mathrm{in} = \frac{1}{g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}} \]

Rin, the resistance the input current has to push through, is one over the total conductance. By Ohm's law a steady input current I raises the voltage by I·Rin, so the leakier the membrane, the smaller every input.

Show the derivation

At rest the voltage does not change, so the three currents add to zero:

\[ g_\mathrm{K}(V - E_\mathrm{K}) + g_\mathrm{Na}(V - E_\mathrm{Na}) + g_\mathrm{Cl}(V - E_\mathrm{Cl}) = 0 \]

Collect the terms with V and solve:

\[ V\,(g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}) = g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}E_\mathrm{Cl} \]

One input

A synapse opens channels that let in positive charge, a current Isyn. As in lesson 2, that current has two places to go: onto the capacitor, which changes the voltage, or out through the resting channels, which does not. Together:

\[ C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = -\,g\,(V - V_\mathrm{rest}) + I_\mathrm{syn}(t), \qquad g = g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl} \]

If the current is steady, V rises toward \(V_\mathrm{rest} + I_\mathrm{syn}\,R_\mathrm{in}\) (Ohm's law again) with time constant \(\tau = C/g\). A brief input charges the capacitor by a fixed amount of charge Q, so the voltage jumps by about \(Q/C\) and then decays back with the same τ:

\[ \Delta V(t) \approx \frac{Q}{C}\,e^{-t/\tau} \]

Temporal summation

Because the voltage decays gradually, a second input that arrives before the first has gone starts from a higher voltage. For a passive membrane the effects add. If inputs come every T, the voltage of one decays by a factor \(e^{-T/\tau}\) between them, so a long train piles up to about

\[ \Delta V_\mathrm{steady} \approx \frac{\Delta V_1}{1 - e^{-T/\tau}} \]

A large τ (large C, small g) means more summation. A short interval T means more too.

Spatial summation

Inputs at different synapses add for a different reason: all their currents flow into the same membrane and charge the same capacitor. Ten synapses at once put ten times the charge on it. In a real cell that membrane is spread out, and a dendrite is a leaky cable, so an input far from the cell body arrives smaller. How far a voltage change spreads along a cable is its length constant λ, set by how leaky the wall is compared with how easily current flows down the inside.

Shunting

An input that opens extra channels adds conductance. An inhibitory Cl⁻ synapse near the cell body, with a battery close to rest, does almost nothing by itself, but it adds conductance for the excitatory current to leak through. That is why inhibition near the cell body can veto the sum.

Show the derivation

With no input, the equation says \(C\,dV/dt = -g(V - V_\mathrm{rest})\), so V relaxes back with \(V - V_\mathrm{rest} \propto e^{-t/\tau}\), \(\tau = C/g\). For a brief input of total charge Q, the capacitor takes the charge at once, so \(\Delta V(0) = Q/C\), and then it decays. Inputs add because the equation is linear in \(V - V_\mathrm{rest}\) (when the driving force of the synapse is nearly constant): if \(\Delta V_1(t)\) solves it for one input, the sum of shifted copies solves it for a train. A train every T gives the geometric series \(\Delta V_1(1 + e^{-T/\tau} + e^{-2T/\tau} + \dots) = \Delta V_1/(1 - e^{-T/\tau})\).

The cell adds up, the initial segment decides

A neuron receives its inputs on its dendrites and cell body, which have few of lesson 3's voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. Those inputs are only local voltage changes that spread along the cell like the cable above: no spike. The sum flows to the axon's first stretch, the axon hillock and axon initial segment (AIS), the first 20 to 60 µm of axon. That is where the cell's output is decided.

Why there

  • Many more Na⁺ channels. The AIS has a very high density, and some are of a type that opens at lower voltage. More Na⁺ channels means the inward current beats the outward currents at a lower voltage (lesson 3's tug of war), so its threshold is the lowest on the cell: the same summed depolarization, a few mV at the cell body, is enough to push the AIS past it.
  • Small, so it charges fast. A patch of thin axon has little capacitance to charge, so a little current moves its voltage a lot. The big cell body is a large capacitor and moves slowly.
  • It sees the sum. The cell body is the junction where all dendrites meet, so its voltage (and the AIS's, one short step away) is the sum of everything the dendrites delivered.

When the AIS fires, the spike runs down the axon as in lesson 4 and also back into the cell body, which is why a recording at the cell body shows the spike a moment after one at the AIS.

What changes the sum

  • Distance: a distal input has to travel along a leaky dendrite, so the closer to the cell body, the more of it arrives (the length constant).
  • Resting conductance: a leakier membrane shortens λ and τ and shrinks every input.
  • Inhibition at the cell body adds a shunt where all the current has to pass.
  • AIS position, length and channel density set where and how easily the spike starts.
