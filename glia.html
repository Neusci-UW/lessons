Glia: the other cells

About half the cells in the brain aren’t neurons. These glial cells don’t fire action potentials, but neurons couldn’t keep signalling without them. Astrocytes clean up after every spike and every synapse: they soak up the K⁺ that firing leaves outside the cells, and they pull glutamate out of the synaptic cleft. Oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells wrap axons in myelin, which speeds the spike along them (The Action Potential Travels). Microglia are the brain’s immune cells. This lesson simulates the astrocytes’ two clean-up jobs, with the Nernst and GHK equations from Membrane Potential and the receptors from The Chemical Synapse.

You'll be able to
  • explain why firing raises [K⁺] outside the cells far more than it lowers [K⁺] inside, and estimate how high it climbs with steady firing
  • predict with the Nernst and GHK equations how a rise in [K⁺] outside moves EK and the neurons’ resting voltage toward threshold
  • explain spatial buffering with Ohm’s law: K⁺ flows into the astrocyte network where it has piled up and out where it is normal
  • explain why glutamate must be cleared within milliseconds, and why blocking its transporters prolongs NMDA currents much more than AMPA currents
  • name the main kinds of glia, what each does, and a disease in which each goes wrong

Theory

In plain words: Every spike lets a little K⁺ out of the neuron. Inside the cell that is a drop in the ocean. Outside, in the thin film of fluid between brain cells, it adds up fast. More K⁺ outside makes EK less negative, so the neurons sit closer to threshold.

A drop inside, a flood outside

To bring the voltage back down after a spike, K⁺ has to leave the cell: at least enough charge to swing the membrane’s capacitor back by about 100 mV. That is about 10−12 mol of K⁺ for each cm² of membrane. For a thin axon 1 µm across, this lowers [K⁺] inside by about 0.04 mM, out of 140 mM: nothing.

The same K⁺ goes into a much smaller space. Brain cells are packed so closely that the fluid between them is only about a fifth of the tissue (Nicholson and Syková), so the space around that axon is a quarter of its own volume. The K⁺ raises [K⁺]o by about 0.17 mM, four times as much as it lowered [K⁺] inside, and on top of only 3 mM: a 6 % rise from a single spike if every fibre nearby fires. The simulation uses 0.05 mM per spike, as if about a third of them fire each time.

Show the numbers

The charge to swing a membrane of capacitance 1 µF/cm² by 0.1 V, turned into moles of K⁺ with Faraday’s constant:

\[ Q = C\,\Delta V = 1\ \mu\mathrm{F/cm^2} \times 0.1\ \mathrm{V} = 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{C/cm^2}, \qquad \frac{Q}{F} = \frac{10^{-7}}{96\,485} \approx 1.0\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{mol/cm^2} \]

A 1 µm length of an axon 1 µm across has 3.1 µm² of membrane (3.1 × 10−8 cm²), so it loses 3.3 × 10−20 mol of K⁺. Its volume is 0.79 µm³ = 7.9 × 10−16 L, and the space around it (a fifth of the tissue, so a quarter of the axon’s volume) is 2.0 × 10−16 L:

\[ \Delta[\mathrm{K^+}]_i = \frac{3.3\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{mol}}{7.9\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{L}} \approx 0.04\ \mathrm{mM}, \qquad \Delta[\mathrm{K^+}]_o = \frac{3.3\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{mol}}{2.0\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{L}} \approx 0.17\ \mathrm{mM} \]

This is the least K⁺ a spike can move. Real spikes move somewhat more, because Na⁺ is still coming in while K⁺ starts going out. Thinner fibres have more membrane for their volume and move [K⁺]o even more.

Why more K⁺ outside depolarizes neurons

This is the Nernst equation from Membrane Potential, working against you. At body temperature RT/F = 26.7 mV, so

\[ E_\mathrm{K} = 26.7\ \mathrm{mV} \times \ln\frac{[\mathrm{K^+}]_o}{[\mathrm{K^+}]_i} \]

With 3 mM outside and 140 mM inside, EK = 26.7 × ln(3/140) = −103 mV. Double [K⁺]o to 6 mM and EK rises by 26.7 × ln 2 = 18.5 mV, to −84 mV. A neuron at rest is mostly a K⁺ electrode, so its resting voltage follows, by less: the GHK equation, with Na⁺ letting in a little current too (PNa/PK = 0.05),

\[ V_\text{rest} = 26.7\ \mathrm{mV} \times \ln\frac{[\mathrm{K^+}]_o + 0.05\,[\mathrm{Na^+}]_o}{[\mathrm{K^+}]_i + 0.05\,[\mathrm{Na^+}]_i} \]

goes from −70 mV at 3 mM to −63 mV at 6 mM, and to −53 mV, past threshold, at 12 mM.

The ceiling, and what happens above it

Normal activity raises [K⁺]o by a millimolar or less. Even very strong stimulation rarely takes it above 10–12 mM (Heinemann and Lux called this the ceiling). Above it, neurons are depolarized enough to fire by themselves, every spike adds more K⁺, and the rise feeds itself: one of the things that turns brain activity into a seizure. In spreading depression, the slow wave behind the migraine aura, [K⁺]o reaches tens of millimolar and the cells stop firing altogether.

Show the model of [K⁺] outside

Firing adds K⁺ at a rate f ΔK (f spikes per second, ΔK = 0.05 mM each). Diffusion away into the rest of the brain removes it with a slow time constant τd = 10 s; astrocytes remove it with a faster one, τa = 1.5 s (at 100 % uptake):

\[ \frac{d[\mathrm{K^+}]_o}{dt} = f\,\Delta K \;-\; \frac{[\mathrm{K^+}]_o - 3\ \mathrm{mM}}{\tau_d} \;-\; \frac{[\mathrm{K^+}]_o - 3\ \mathrm{mM}}{\tau_a} \]

It is a leaky bucket, the same as charging the membrane’s capacitor in Passive Properties: Time. With steady firing [K⁺]o settles where removal equals input, and gets there with the combined time constant τ:

\[ \Delta[\mathrm{K^+}]_o = f\,\Delta K\,\tau, \qquad \frac{1}{\tau} = \frac{1}{\tau_d} + \frac{1}{\tau_a} \]

That is Ohm’s law, V = I R, in another form: a steady input times a time constant. At 20 Hz: f ΔK = 1 mM/s, τ = 1/(1/10 + 1/1.5) = 1.30 s, so the rise is 1.3 mM, to 4.3 mM, reached within a few seconds. Without the astrocytes τ = 10 s: the rise would level off 10 mM above rest, at 13 mM, but 10 s of firing gets only 63 % of the way there, to 9.3 mM.

In plain words: Astrocytes are leaky to K⁺ and almost nothing else, and they are joined into one big network. Where K⁺ piles up outside, it flows into the network; it flows back out wherever K⁺ is normal, or into the blood. The network works like a wire that carries the extra K⁺ away.

A cell that is almost all K⁺ channels

An astrocyte’s membrane is packed with one kind of channel open at rest, the inward-rectifier K⁺ channel Kir4.1, and has almost no others. So its voltage sits close to EK, about −90 mV, and follows EK when [K⁺] around it changes. Orkand, Nicholls and Kuffler found exactly this: when nerve impulses raised the K⁺ around glial cells, the glia depolarized by the amount the Nernst equation predicts.

Ohm’s law at both ends

Astrocytes are connected to their neighbours by gap junctions, channels that join one cell’s inside to the next (as in electrical synapses), so a whole network of them shares one voltage V. Its membrane near the firing neurons faces high [K⁺]o; its membrane far away faces normal 3 mM. Each patch passes a K⁺ current by Ohm’s law, conductance times driving force:

\[ I_\text{near} = g_\text{near}\,\bigl(V - E_\mathrm{K,near}\bigr), \qquad I_\text{far} = g_\text{far}\,\bigl(V - E_\mathrm{K,far}\bigr) \]

Near the neurons EK has risen above V, so the current is negative: K⁺ flows in. Far away EK is still low, below V, so K⁺ flows out. The network settles where the two currents cancel, at the conductance-weighted average of the two batteries, the same chord-conductance formula as the resting voltage in Membrane Potential:

\[ V = \frac{g_\text{near}E_\mathrm{K,near} + g_\text{far}E_\mathrm{K,far}}{g_\text{near} + g_\text{far}} \]

A worked number. Take 100 mM K⁺ inside the astrocyte, [K⁺]o = 6 mM near the neurons and 3 mM far away, and equal conductances. EK,near = 26.7 × ln(6/100) = −75 mV and EK,far = 26.7 × ln(3/100) = −94 mV, so V = −84 mV. Near the neurons the driving force is −84 − (−75) = −9 mV, so K⁺ flows in; far away it is +9 mV, so the same K⁺ current flows out. In the simulation the astrocyte’s voltage is this V, and the dashed line is EK,near.

Where the K⁺ goes

The far end may be quiet tissue, or an astrocyte endfoot on a blood vessel. In the retina, Müller glial cells siphon K⁺ from the active layers to the fluid inside the eye (Newman and colleagues). Astrocytes also take up K⁺ with their Na⁺/K⁺ pumps and cotransporters, and in the end the neurons’ own Na⁺/K⁺ pumps take it all back.

When it fails

People with mutations in the gene for Kir4.1 (KCNJ10) have EAST syndrome: epilepsy, ataxia (poor coordination), sensorineural deafness and a kidney salt-wasting tubulopathy, wherever Kir4.1 does its job. In mice whose glia lack Kir4.1, K⁺ released by activity is cleared more slowly. The simulation’s 0 % switches off all astrocyte uptake, which is more drastic than losing Kir4.1 alone.

Show where the astrocytes’ time constant comes from

With equal conductances g near and far, V is halfway between the two EK’s, and the K⁺ current into the astrocyte near the neurons is

\[ -I_\text{near} = g\,\bigl(E_\mathrm{K,near} - V\bigr) = \frac{g}{2}\,\bigl(E_\mathrm{K,near} - E_\mathrm{K,far}\bigr) = \frac{g}{2}\times 26.7\ \mathrm{mV}\times\ln\frac{[\mathrm{K^+}]_o}{3\ \mathrm{mM}} \]

For small rises, ln(1 + x) ≈ x, so ln([K⁺]o/3 mM) ≈ ([K⁺]o − 3 mM)/3 mM: the uptake is close to proportional to the rise in [K⁺]o. Uptake proportional to the excess is what a time constant means:

\[ \text{uptake} = \frac{[\mathrm{K^+}]_o - 3\ \mathrm{mM}}{\tau_a} \]

More Kir4.1 channels (bigger g), or a bigger network to share the K⁺ with, make τa shorter. The simulation uses this straight-line form, with τa = 1.5 s at 100 % uptake.

Only diffusion is left.

In plain words: Glutamate isn’t broken down in the cleft. It spreads out of the cleft within a millisecond, and transporters packed on the astrocyte wrapped around the synapse grab it before it can reach other synapses. That keeps each synapse’s message short and private. Block the transporters and a little glutamate lingers: too little for AMPA receptors, plenty for NMDA receptors.

Transporters, not enzymes

Acetylcholine is split by an enzyme in the cleft (The Chemical Synapse). Glutamate is instead carried into cells by transporters, mostly two on astrocytes: EAAT1 (GLAST) and EAAT2 (GLT-1), which does most of the brain’s glutamate uptake. Each glutamate is carried in together with 3 Na⁺ and 1 H⁺ while 1 K⁺ goes out, so the Na⁺ gradient that the Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains powers the uptake. Bergles and Jahr recorded these transporters’ currents in astrocytes every time a nearby synapse released glutamate. Inside the astrocyte glutamate becomes glutamine, which goes back to the neurons to be made into glutamate again.

First diffusion, then capture

One vesicle raises glutamate in the cleft to about 1 mM, and diffusion out of the narrow cleft brings it down again with a time constant of about 1 ms (Clements and colleagues). What happens to it next is up to the transporters. There are so many of them around the synapse that they bind glutamate within a few milliseconds, before much of it can drift to the next synapse. In the simulation, the cleft’s glutamate falls with τ = 1 ms in every case; what the transporters change is the small tail that is left: about 5 µM for 2 ms with them, 50 µM for 30 ms without.

Why NMDA receptors notice and AMPA receptors don’t

A receptor binds glutamate at a rate proportional to the concentration, and lets go at a fixed rate. The fraction r of receptors open follows

\[ \frac{dr}{dt} = \alpha\,[\mathrm{Glu}]\,(1 - r) - \beta\,r \]

NMDA receptors bind slowly but hold on: α = 0.072 per mM per ms, β = 0.0066 per ms, so once bound they stay for about 1/β = 150 ms. AMPA receptors bind and let go quickly (α = 1.1 per mM per ms, β = 0.19 per ms), and need two glutamates bound to open, so in the simulation their binding goes as [Glu]².

A worked number. Hold glutamate at 10 µM (0.01 mM), as it might be with the transporters blocked. With r steady, binding equals unbinding, α[Glu](1 − r) = βr, so r = α[Glu] / (α[Glu] + β). For NMDA: 0.072 × 0.01 = 0.00072, and r = 0.00072 / (0.00072 + 0.0066) = 10 %. For AMPA: 1.1 × 0.01² = 0.00011, and r = 0.00011 / (0.00011 + 0.19) = 0.06 %. The same lingering glutamate opens over a hundred times more NMDA receptors than AMPA receptors. That is why blocking uptake with TBOA prolongs NMDA currents and lets glutamate from one synapse reach NMDA receptors at its neighbours, while AMPA currents barely change (Arnth-Jensen, Jabaudon and Scanziani).

When clearing fails

NMDA receptors let in Ca²⁺. Kept open too long, as when glutamate is not cleared, they let in so much that the cell dies: excitotoxicity. It is part of the damage in stroke, where cells run out of energy, the Na⁺ gradient runs down and the transporters stop working. In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) the astrocytes’ EAAT2 is often lost in the motor cortex and spinal cord.

In plain words: Glia are as numerous as neurons. Astrocytes keep the space around neurons clean and link them to the blood; oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells insulate axons with myelin; microglia defend the brain and tidy up its connections.

How many?

Textbooks used to say glia outnumber neurons ten to one. Counting the cells of whole human brains gave about 86 billion neurons and about as many other cells (Azevedo and colleagues), so glia are about half the cells, not nine-tenths.

Myelin

Oligodendrocytes in the brain and spinal cord, and Schwann cells in the nerves, wrap axons in many layers of membrane. That cuts the axon’s capacitance and leak, so a spike jumps from one gap in the myelin to the next and travels many times faster: The Action Potential Travels shows it. In multiple sclerosis the immune system attacks central myelin; in Guillain–Barré syndrome, usually after an infection, it attacks peripheral nerves.

Microglia

Microglia are not made from nervous tissue at all: they come from blood-forming cells of the embryo’s yolk sac and settle in the brain before birth (Ginhoux and colleagues). They constantly feel around with their processes, clear dead cells and debris, and during development they engulf weak synapses that are not used, sharpening the brain’s wiring (Schafer and colleagues).
