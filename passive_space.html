Passive properties: space

Lesson 2 charged one small patch of membrane. A real neuron is long: its dendrites and axon are thin tubes, and current put in at one place has to travel along the inside to reach the rest. On the way some of it leaks out through the membrane. Join lesson 2's patches in a row and see how far a voltage change spreads (the length constant, λ) and how that combines with how fast each patch charges (the time constant, τ).

You'll be able to
  • describe a dendrite or axon as lesson 2's RC patches in a row, joined by the resistance of the cytoplasm
  • explain why a steady voltage change shrinks with distance, by e (to 37%) every length constant, λ = √(rm/ri)
  • predict how diameter, membrane leak and cytoplasm change λ: wider and less leaky spread farther
  • explain why a signal far away arrives smaller, later and slower: space and time together

Theory

In plain words: A dendrite or axon is a long, thin tube of membrane filled with salty cytoplasm. Treat each short piece of it as lesson 2's RC patch, and join neighbouring pieces by the resistance of the cytoplasm between them. Current put in at one piece then has three places to go: onto that piece's capacitor, out through its membrane, or along the inside to the next piece.

Lesson 2's patch, in a row

In lesson 2 a single patch obeyed \(C\,dV/dt = -g_m (V - E) + I\): what comes in either charges the capacitor or leaks out. Now join patches in a row. Between patch \(k\) and its neighbours the cytoplasm is a resistor, and by Ohm's law the current through it is the voltage difference divided by its resistance. So each patch gets two more terms, one per neighbour:

\[ C\,\frac{dV_k}{dt} = -\,g_m\,(V_k - E) + g_c\,(V_{k-1} - V_k) + g_c\,(V_{k+1} - V_k) \]

Here \(g_c\) is the cytoplasm's conductance between two neighbours, per cm² of membrane. It is large when the tube is wide (more cytoplasm to carry the current) and the patches are close together.

Charge flows from high to low

The cytoplasm carries current from the patch whose inside is more positive to the less positive one. So a patch that has been charged up shares its charge with its neighbours, who share it with theirs, while every patch leaks some out through its membrane. That leak is why the change gets smaller the farther it goes.

From patches to a smooth cable: the cable equation

Make the patches shorter and shorter, \(\Delta x \to 0\). The two neighbour terms become a second derivative, \((V_{k-1} - 2V_k + V_{k+1}) / \Delta x^2 \to \partial^2 V/\partial x^2\). Written per unit length of cable, with \(r_m\) the membrane's resistance times a unit length (Ω·cm), \(r_i\) the cytoplasm's resistance per unit length (Ω/cm) and \(c_m\) the capacitance per unit length:

\[ \frac{1}{r_i}\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} = \frac{V}{r_m} + c_m\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \]

(V measured from rest.) This is the cable equation, first worked out for the transatlantic telegraph cable and applied to nerves by Hodgkin and Rushton. Multiplying both sides by \(r_m\): \(\lambda^2\,\partial^2 V/\partial x^2 = V + \tau\,\partial V/\partial t\), with \(\lambda^2 = r_m/r_i\) and \(\tau = r_m c_m\).

In plain words: Keep a steady current flowing into patch 1 and wait. Each patch along the cable ends up a fixed fraction of the one before, because at every step some of the current escapes through the membrane. The length constant λ is how far you go before the change has fallen to about a third. Current spreads farther when it flows easily along the inside (a wide tube) and escapes with difficulty through the wall (a membrane with little leak).

An exponential fall with distance

At steady state nothing changes with time, and in a long cable the voltage falls off exponentially with distance \(x\) from where the current goes in:

\[ V(x) = V_0\,e^{-x/\lambda}, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{r_m}{r_i}} = \sqrt{\frac{d\,R_m}{4\,R_i}} \]

Every λ along, the voltage change is multiplied by \(1/e\), about 0.37: to 37% at λ, 14% at 2λ, 5% at 3λ. \(R_m\) is the membrane's resistance for 1 cm² (1/gm), \(R_i\) the cytoplasm's resistivity, and \(d\) the diameter.

A quick number

With \(d = 1.5\) µm, \(R_m = 1/(0.5\ \text{mS/cm}^2) = 2000\ \Omega\,\text{cm}^2\) and \(R_i = 150\ \Omega\,\text{cm}\): \(\lambda = \sqrt{1.5\times10^{-4} \times 2000 / 600}\ \text{cm} \approx 0.022\ \text{cm} = 224\ \mu\text{m}\). The circuit's defaults.

What sets it

  • Diameter: a wider tube has more cytoplasm to carry current (ri falls as d²) but also more membrane to leak through (rm falls as d). The first wins, so λ grows as √d: four times the diameter, twice the λ.
  • Membrane leak: more open channels, more escape: λ falls as 1/√gm.
  • Cytoplasm: a more resistive cytoplasm passes less on: λ falls as 1/√Ri.
Show the derivation

At steady state \(\partial V/\partial t = 0\), so the cable equation is \(\lambda^2 V'' = V\). Functions equal to their own second derivative (times a constant) are exponentials: \(V = A e^{-x/\lambda} + B e^{x/\lambda}\). In a long cable V cannot grow without limit, so \(B = 0\) and \(V = V_0 e^{-x/\lambda}\).

For a cylinder of diameter \(d\): one unit length has membrane area \(\pi d\), so \(r_m = R_m/(\pi d)\); its cross-section is \(\pi d^2/4\), so \(r_i = 4R_i/(\pi d^2)\). Their ratio is \(r_m/r_i = d R_m/(4 R_i)\).

The circuit is the same thing in steps of \(\Delta x\) = 50 µm: its neighbour conductance per cm² of membrane is \(g_c = d/(4 R_i \Delta x^2)\), and \(\lambda/\Delta x = \sqrt{g_c/g_m}\). A short cable sealed at its far end holds a little more voltage near the end than the exponential, as the dashed and dotted lines in the circuit's chart show.

Four times the leak: λ halves.

In plain words: Each patch still has to charge its capacitor, as in lesson 2, and it can only start once charge has reached it from the patches before. So a voltage change far along the cable arrives later, rises more slowly and ends up smaller. The cable blurs signals in time as well as shrinking them in space: a quick blip fades faster with distance than a long, steady push.

Two constants, one cable

The cable equation has both: \(\lambda^2\,\partial^2 V/\partial x^2 = V + \tau\,\partial V/\partial t\). τ is lesson 2's time constant, \(C/g_m\), the same for every patch: it does not depend on the diameter. λ is set by the cable's shape and leak. Together they set how fast a change spreads: the point where the voltage has risen halfway to its final value moves along the cable at about two λ every τ.

Later, slower, smaller

  • At the input, the voltage rises fastest. It gets halfway to its final value in about a quarter of τ, where a lone patch would take about ⅔ of τ. It settles lower than a lone patch, because much of its current flows on along the cable instead of charging it, so it has less far to go.
  • One λ away, the voltage ends at only 37% of the input's final change, and is slower to get there: halfway in about ¾ of τ.
  • Two λ away, it ends at 14%, halfway there only after about 1¼ τ: the signal has to charge every stretch of membrane on the way.

A short pulse never lets the far patches charge up before it ends, so it shrinks with distance even faster than a steady current. The cable is a low-pass filter: slow signals get through, fast ones are smoothed out.

At the cell body: inputs from farther out arrive smaller, later and broader.

In plain words: λ and τ decide how far and how fast a voltage change spreads along a neuron without any help. In dendrites that decides how much of a distant input reaches the cell body. In axons it decides how quickly each stretch ahead of an action potential is charged to threshold, and so how fast the spike travels; myelin is a way to make λ much longer.

Dendrites

Synaptic inputs land all over a dendritic tree, often hundreds of µm from the cell body, and much of the time the voltage they cause spreads passively, as here (many dendrites also have voltage-gated channels, which this lesson leaves out): one far out arrives at the cell body smaller and later than one close in. Lesson 6 follows many such inputs adding up.

Axons

An action potential (lesson 4) uses this same spread: the current from an active patch flows ahead along the inside and charges the next patch to threshold. The farther the current reaches (large λ) and the faster each patch charges (small τ), the faster the spike moves. Wider axons are faster for this reason: λ grows with √d.

Myelin

Myelin wraps the axon in many layers of membrane. That multiplies rm (far less leak) and divides the capacitance, so λ becomes many times longer: current from one node of Ranvier reaches the next before it leaks away. Diseases that strip myelin, such as multiple sclerosis, shorten λ again, and signals slow or fail.
