From one cell to the next: the chemical synapse

A spike (lesson 4) runs to the end of the axon, but it cannot jump to the next cell: there is a gap, the synaptic cleft. Instead the spike opens Ca²⁺ channels, the Ca²⁺ that rushes in makes small bubbles of transmitter (vesicles) fuse with the membrane, and the transmitter crosses the gap and opens channels on the next cell. Those channels are one more conductance with its own battery, so, exactly as in lessons 1 and 2, they pull the next cell's voltage toward that battery.

You'll be able to
  • put the steps in order, spike → Ca²⁺ entry → vesicle fusion → transmitter in the cleft → receptor channels open → current → voltage change, and say what blocks each one
  • explain why release depends so steeply on Ca²⁺ outside the cell, and why each spike releases a different number of vesicles
  • calculate a synaptic current with Ohm's law, Isyn = gsyn(V − Erev), and find the reversal potential of a channel that passes both Na⁺ and K⁺
  • decide whether a synapse excites or inhibits from where its reversal potential sits compared with rest and threshold, not from the name of its transmitter
  • explain how a synapse's signal is ended

Simulation

Start here
  1. Watch the spike open the Ca²⁺ channels, a few vesicles fuse, transmitter cross the cleft and AMPA channels open: a small EPSP in the next cell. Fire it again: a different number of vesicles each time.
  2. The spike still arrives, but nothing is released.
  3. Ca²⁺ left over from one spike helps the next release more, until the ready vesicles run out.
  4. A GABAA synapse opens Cl⁻ channels. Their battery is below rest, so the next cell moves down, away from threshold.
  5. Hold the next cell at a range of voltages: the AMPA current shrinks to nothing at 0 mV and flips sign beyond it.

The capacitor C stores charge; the resting conductance grest with its battery Erest holds the cell at rest; the synapse adds a second conductance, gsyn, with its own battery, the reversal potential Erev. The synaptic resistor darkens as more receptor channels open.
  • Arrows: the current through each branch, pointing up when positive charge flows into the cell. Their length shows the size.
  • Voltage clamp: an amplifier holds V fixed at the holding voltage by supplying whatever current the synapse lets through, so the synaptic current can be measured directly. What the voltage does when nothing holds it is in Part 1.
  • Post: voltage (whole cell): the next cell is treated as one point, as in lesson 2: spine, dendrite and soma always share one voltage, which is what an electrode at the soma would read if the cell were compact. In a real neuron the EPSP is biggest and fastest at the spine; by the time it reaches the soma it is smaller and slower, smoothed out along the dendrite (lesson 3 shows this; lessons 6 and 7 build on it). For the same reason, a clamp at a real soma cannot fully hold a distant spine, so Erev measured that way comes out a little off.
  • Charts: in voltage clamp each Fire adds a trace, labelled with its command voltage VC (the holding voltage the amplifier imposes), until you press Clear. Sweep does this for you: it steps the holding voltage from the lowest to the highest VC in the number of steps you set, and fires once at each, so the family of currents builds up trace by trace. The dashed line on the voltage chart is Erev; the dotted line is rest.
  • I–V chart (under the circuit): one dot per Fire, at its holding voltage: the trace's largest current, marked on the current chart; Sweep fills in the curve, and the ring marks the trace running now. Where the dots cross zero is Erev (dashed): the channels open there but pass no net current. I–V / V–I swaps the axes.
  • Muscarinic (closes K⁺): acetylcholine acting through a G protein closes K⁺ channels that were open at rest. The conductance goes down, yet the cell depolarizes: less K⁺ leaves, so the pull toward EK weakens. It is slow (hundreds of ms, so these traces are 800 ms long) and its current reverses at EK, about −90 mV, not at 0 mV: sweep the holding voltage and only the −100 mV trace comes out the other way. Because the membrane gets less leaky, the same input moves V more while it lasts.
  • The conductance gsyn is the same whatever the voltage; the current is not. It is gsyn times the driving force V − Erev, so it is large far from Erev, zero at Erev, and reverses beyond it. Sweep the holding voltage to see it.
  • The sign of the current tells you which way the synapse would push V if it were free (Part 1): inward (negative) pushes V up toward Erev, outward pushes it down. AMPA (Erev = 0 mV) is inward at any V below 0; GABAA and glycine (Erev ≈ −80 mV) are outward at rest.
  • Move Erev to −65 mV, a little above rest: the synapse now depolarizes the cell, but its pull stops well below threshold. That is shunting inhibition: it also makes the membrane leakier, so excitatory inputs move V less.

Units

Real units

mV
millivolts, for voltage. V is the inside measured relative to the outside.
ms
milliseconds, for time. Transmitter is released and cleared in about a millisecond; AMPA currents last a few ms.
mM, µM
millimolar and micromolar, for concentration. Ca²⁺ is about 2 mM outside a cell but only 0.1 µM inside at rest, 20 000 times less; near an open Ca²⁺ channel it briefly reaches tens of µM.

For one synapse on one cell

nS
nanosiemens, for conductance (1/resistance). One receptor channel is about 10–50 pS (picosiemens); a synapse's open channels together reach about 1 nS.
pA
picoamps, for current. 1 nS × 70 mV = 70 pA. Negative when positive charge flows into the cell.
pF
picofarads, for the whole cell's capacitance (lesson 2's µF/cm² times its area). pA / pF = mV/ms: 50 pA into 50 pF changes V by 1 mV every ms.

Theory

In plain words: When the spike reaches the end of the axon, it opens Ca²⁺ channels. Ca²⁺ rushes in and makes vesicles, small bubbles full of transmitter, fuse with the membrane and empty into the gap. Chance decides how many: each spike releases a different number, and a little less Ca²⁺ means far less release.

Ca²⁺ is the trigger

The membrane of the axon's terminal has voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, packed into the active zone right next to the docked vesicles. They open when the spike depolarizes the terminal. Ca²⁺ has a huge driving force into the cell: about 2 mM outside against 0.1 µM inside, so its Nernst potential is about +130 mV, and at any voltage the cell reaches Ca²⁺ flows in.

\[ E_\mathrm{Ca} = \frac{RT}{2F}\ln\frac{[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_\mathrm{out}}{[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_\mathrm{in}} \approx 12.9\ \mathrm{mV} \times \ln\frac{2\ \mathrm{mM}}{0.0001\ \mathrm{mM}} \approx +128\ \mathrm{mV} \]

The Ca²⁺ that comes in stays near the channel for well under a millisecond before it spreads out and is pumped away, so only vesicles docked right beside the channels are triggered. Ca²⁺ binds a sensor protein on each vesicle (synaptotagmin), which pulls the vesicle and the cell membranes together until they fuse and the transmitter spills into the cleft.

Why release is so steep

A vesicle fuses only when several Ca²⁺ ions, about four, are bound to its sensor at once. So the rate of release goes roughly as the fourth power of the Ca²⁺ concentration:

\[ \text{release rate} \propto [\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]^4 \]

Halve the Ca²⁺ and release falls by about 24 = 16 times. This is why Ca²⁺ outside the cell matters so much for transmission, and why a drug that blocks only some of the terminal's Ca²⁺ channels can stop a synapse almost completely.

Quanta: release comes in packets

Each vesicle holds a few thousand transmitter molecules, and a vesicle either fuses or does not. So the response of the next cell comes in steps of one vesicle's worth, a quantum of size q. Each of the n docked vesicles fuses with some probability p, so a spike releases on average n·p vesicles, and the average response is

\[ \text{average response} = n \cdot p \cdot q \]

The number released changes from spike to spike by chance, which is why the same spike gives EPSPs of different sizes, and sometimes none at all.

Show where the steps and failures come from

If each of the n docked vesicles fuses independently with probability p, the number that fuse, k, follows the binomial distribution:

\[ P(k) = \binom{n}{k}\,p^k\,(1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad P(0) = (1-p)^n \]

With n = 10 and p = 0.3, P(0) = 0.710 ≈ 3%: about one spike in thirty releases nothing (a failure). Lower Ca²⁺ outside lowers p, and failures become common. Del Castillo and Katz used exactly this, at the frog neuromuscular junction in low Ca²⁺, to show that release is quantal.

Facilitation and depression

Some Ca²⁺ is still left in the terminal tens of milliseconds after a spike. A second spike soon after adds its Ca²⁺ on top, and because of the fourth power, a little extra Ca²⁺ raises release a lot: facilitation. But each fused vesicle leaves an empty docking site that takes a few hundred milliseconds to refill, so a fast train eventually runs out of ready vesicles: depression. Which wins depends on p: synapses with low p facilitate, synapses with high p depress.

In plain words: The receptors on the next cell are channels. When transmitter binds, they open and let particular ions through, and which ions they pass decides what they do to the cell.

Receptors that are channels

The receptors in this lesson are ionotropic: the receptor is itself an ion channel, and binding transmitter opens it within a fraction of a millisecond. Other receptors are metabotropic (G-protein-coupled): binding starts a chain of reactions inside the cell that may open or close channels later, over tens of milliseconds to seconds. Most transmitters have both kinds.

A receptor's speed comes from how long transmitter stays bound and the channel stays open. AMPA lets go of glutamate in a millisecond or two; GABAA holds on for several times longer. In Part 2, the Decay τ slider (how long the channels stay open) sets that time.

In plain words: An open receptor channel is just another channel: it pulls the next cell's voltage toward its own target, its reversal potential. A channel that passes both Na⁺ and K⁺ has a target in between their two targets, about 0 mV.

Ohm's law again

An open receptor channel is a conductance like any other (lesson 1), and the current through it is the conductance times the driving force, the distance from the voltage to the channel's battery:

\[ I_\mathrm{syn} = g_\mathrm{syn}(t)\,\bigl(V - E_\mathrm{rev}\bigr) \]

The transmitter sets gsyn(t): it rises as channels open and falls as they close. The battery Erev, the reversal potential, is set by which ions the channel lets through. At V = Erev the channel can be wide open and pass no net current; past it, the current reverses. That is how Erev is measured: voltage-clamp the cell at several voltages and find where the current changes sign.

A channel for two ions

AMPA and nicotinic channels let through both Na⁺ (ENa ≈ +60 mV) and K⁺ (EK ≈ −90 mV). Inside the channel the two ions are two conductances side by side, each pulling toward its own battery, and the channel settles where their currents cancel: the conductance-weighted average, the same chord-conductance formula as for the resting voltage in lesson 1.

\[ E_\mathrm{rev} = \frac{g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K}}{g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}} \]

With gNa about 1.5 times gK, Erev = (1.5 × 60 − 90) / 2.5 = 0 mV. Use the calculator below to try other mixes.

Show the derivation

At the reversal potential the Na⁺ and K⁺ currents through the channel add to zero:

\[ g_\mathrm{Na}(E_\mathrm{rev} - E_\mathrm{Na}) + g_\mathrm{K}(E_\mathrm{rev} - E_\mathrm{K}) = 0 \]

Collect the terms with Erev:

\[ E_\mathrm{rev}\,(g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}) = g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K} \]

and divide. Below Erev more Na⁺ flows in than K⁺ flows out, a net inward current; above it the opposite.

From current to voltage

In the next cell, the synaptic current joins lesson 2's circuit: it either charges the capacitor, moving V, or leaks back out through the resting conductance.

\[ C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = -\,g_\mathrm{rest}(V - E_\mathrm{rest}) \;-\; g_\mathrm{syn}(t)\,(V - E_\mathrm{rev}) \]

So V moves from rest toward Erev, but only part of the way: gsyn is small and brief, and the resting conductance pulls back. A single synapse typically moves V by well under a millivolt to a few millivolts. Summation (lesson 6) is how many of them add up.

In plain words: Whether a synapse excites or inhibits depends on where its target voltage sits. A target above threshold pushes the cell toward firing; a target below threshold holds it back.

It is the battery that decides

A synapse pulls V toward its Erev. Whether that excites or inhibits depends on where Erev sits compared with the threshold for a spike (about −55 mV), not on the transmitter's name:

  • Erev above threshold (AMPA, nicotinic: 0 mV): the synapse pushes V up toward threshold. An excitatory postsynaptic potential, EPSP.
  • Erev below rest (GABAA, glycine in a mature neuron: about −80 mV): V is pulled down, away from threshold. An inhibitory postsynaptic potential, IPSP.
  • Erev between rest and threshold: V moves up a little, yet the synapse still inhibits. It cannot push V past its own Erev, which is below threshold, and while its channels are open the membrane is leakier, so excitatory currents leak away instead of charging the capacitor. This is shunting inhibition.

The same transmitter can do different things in different cells. Young neurons keep more Cl⁻ inside, so their ECl is above rest and GABA depolarizes them; as they mature they start pumping Cl⁻ out and GABA becomes inhibitory.

In plain words: To be ready for the next signal, the transmitter has to be cleared from the gap: it drifts away, is pumped back into cells, or is broken down. Many drugs work by slowing this clean-up.

Clearing the cleft

For a synapse to signal the next spike, the transmitter from this one has to go. Three things remove it:

  • Diffusion out of the narrow cleft, about 20 nm wide.
  • Uptake: transporters in the terminal and in nearby glial cells pump glutamate, GABA, dopamine, serotonin and noradrenaline back in. Many drugs act here: SSRI antidepressants block serotonin uptake, and cocaine blocks dopamine uptake, so the transmitter lingers.
  • Breakdown: at the neuromuscular junction acetylcholinesterase splits acetylcholine within a millisecond. Anticholinesterases (neostigmine, used in myasthenia gravis; nerve agents, as poisons) slow this, so acetylcholine keeps acting.

How long the current lasts is set by whichever is slower: clearing the transmitter or the receptor letting go of it. AMPA receptors let go fast, so clearing matters.

Keeping the terminal going

A fused vesicle's membrane is taken back into the terminal (endocytosis), refilled with transmitter and docked again, which takes from seconds to tens of seconds; a reserve pool of full vesicles covers the gap. Ca²⁺ is pumped back out of the terminal, so the next spike starts from a low Ca²⁺ again.
