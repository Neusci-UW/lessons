Voltage-gated channels: the Hodgkin–Huxley model

In lesson 2 you opened the Na⁺ channels with a switch, and the membrane charged like a capacitor toward a new voltage. In a real neuron nobody flips that switch: the voltage itself opens the channels. Replace the switch with gates that respond to voltage, as Hodgkin and Huxley did in 1952, and the same circuit fires an action potential: the spike you watched travel in lesson 4, taken apart in one patch of membrane.

You'll be able to
  • describe a voltage-gated channel as lesson 2's resistor and battery whose conductance depends on the voltage: gNa = ḡNam³h and gK = ḡKn⁴
  • explain threshold: the point where the Na⁺ channels that depolarization opens let in more current than the K⁺ channels let out, so the depolarization feeds itself
  • explain why a spike is all-or-none and why it ends by itself: Na⁺ channels inactivate (h) and K⁺ channels open (n)
  • predict how the conductances and Nernst potentials change the resting voltage, the threshold and the spike

Simulation

Start here
  1. Open the Na⁺ channels by hand: the voltage climbs to a new steady value, V∞, and stays there until you close them.
  2. Replace the switch with gates. A short pulse of current depolarizes the membrane (makes the inside less negative) and the Na⁺ channels open by themselves. Most then close again (inactivation), but the voltage gets stuck high.
  3. The complete active membrane, Hodgkin and Huxley's model: K⁺ channels open late and bring the voltage down fast. An action potential.
  4. A smaller pulse stops short of threshold: the voltage just leaks back, like lesson 2's RC circuit.
  5. With TTX, the pufferfish toxin that blocks the voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, even a large pulse only charges the capacitor.

Theory

In plain words: In lesson 2 you opened the Na⁺ channels by hand. In a real neuron the channels open and close by themselves as the voltage changes. That makes a loop: the voltage changes the channels, the channels change the current, and the current changes the voltage. This loop is all it takes to make an action potential.

Lesson 2's circuit

Each channel type is a resistor and a battery, so its current follows Ohm's law with the driving force V − E, and the capacitor takes whatever the channels don't:

\[ C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = -\,g_\mathrm{Na}\,(V - E_\mathrm{Na}) - g_\mathrm{K}\,(V - E_\mathrm{K}) - g_\mathrm{Cl}\,(V - E_\mathrm{Cl}) \]

With fixed g's, V moves exponentially to the conductance-weighted average of the batteries, \(V_\infty\), with time constant \(\tau = C/(g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Cl})\). At rest K⁺ has by far the largest g, so V sits near EK, pulled a little up by the small Na⁺ leak. Open the Na⁺ channels and it stays at the new \(V_\infty\) for as long as they are open.

The one change

Hodgkin and Huxley kept that equation and changed one thing: alongside the leaks, there are Na⁺ and K⁺ channels whose g is not a fixed number but depends on the voltage, and on how long it has been there.

\[ C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = -\,\bar g_\mathrm{Na}\,m^3 h\,(V - E_\mathrm{Na}) \;-\; \bar g_\mathrm{K}\,n^4\,(V - E_\mathrm{K}) \;-\; I_\mathrm{leak} \;+\; I_\mathrm{stim} \]

where Ileak is lesson 2's three leaks together:

\[ I_\mathrm{leak} = g_\mathrm{K,leak}\,(V - E_\mathrm{K}) + g_\mathrm{Na,leak}\,(V - E_\mathrm{Na}) + g_\mathrm{Cl}\,(V - E_\mathrm{Cl}) \]

\(\bar g\) (“g-bar”) is the conductance if every channel were open. The gates m, h and n are numbers between 0 and 1 that open and close as the voltage changes. That is the whole model: lesson 2's circuit with resistors that the voltage turns up and down.

The result is a loop the switch never had. The voltage sets the gates, the gates set the currents, and the currents set the voltage.

In plain words: Each channel has gates, parts of the protein that open or close as the voltage changes. A Na⁺ channel has a fast gate that opens when the cell depolarizes (m) and a slower one that then plugs it (h). A K⁺ channel has one slow gate (n). That difference in speed, Na⁺ on fast and off slowly while K⁺ comes on slowly, gives the spike its shape.

Three kinds of gate

  • m, the Na⁺ activation gate, opens when V rises, and fast: in a fraction of a millisecond. A channel needs three m gates open, so its share is m³.
  • h, the Na⁺ inactivation gate, does the opposite: it closes when V rises, and slowly. Even with its m gates open, a channel whose h has closed is plugged. So gNa = ḡNam³h: Na⁺ flows only in the window after m has opened and before h has closed.
  • n, the K⁺ gate, opens when V rises, slowly, like h. Four are needed (n⁴). K⁺ channels do not inactivate.

How a gate moves

For every voltage each gate has a value it heads toward, \(x_\infty(V)\), and a time constant, \(\tau_x(V)\), just like lesson 2's capacitor heading to \(V_\infty\):

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{x_\infty(V) - x}{\tau_x(V)}, \qquad x = m,\ h,\ n \]

So at a steady voltage each gate relaxes exponentially, but the target and the speed change whenever V does. m's τ is about ten times shorter than h's and n's; that difference in speed is what makes a spike.

Hodgkin and Huxley's form: opening and closing rates

They wrote each gate as many independent gates flipping open at a rate α(V) and shut at a rate β(V) (per ms). The fraction open changes by the gates opening minus the gates closing:

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = \alpha_x(V)\,(1 - x) - \beta_x(V)\,x \]

which is the same equation with \(x_\infty = \alpha/(\alpha + \beta)\) and \(\tau_x = 1/(\alpha + \beta)\). They fitted α and β for each gate to their voltage-clamp recordings; the voltage-clamp calculator below uses them, at the temperature of the cell picked in the simulation: 6.3 °C for the squid axon, as they measured; 20 °C for the frog fibre; and 25 °C for the mammalian neuron, with the K⁺ gate 10 mV more positive, as in mammalian neurons. The gates speed up about 3× for every 10 °C, so at 25 °C they are 7.8× faster than in the squid experiments.

In plain words: A small push up is pulled back down, mostly by K⁺ leaving the cell. A bigger push opens enough Na⁺ channels that the Na⁺ coming in wins, and the rise then feeds itself. The voltage where Na⁺ starts to win is the threshold. Past it the cell always gives the same full spike: all-or-none.

A tug of war

Push V up a little from rest. Two things happen at once. The K⁺ current outward grows, because V is now further from EK: that pulls V back down (lesson 2). And a few more m gates open, so the Na⁺ current inward grows, which pushes V further up.

For a small push, the K⁺ current wins and V returns to rest. But m opens steeply with voltage, and each mV opens more of them than the last. At some voltage the extra Na⁺ current inward overtakes the extra K⁺ current outward. From there the rise feeds itself: more V, more m, more Na⁺, more V. That voltage is the threshold.

All-or-none

Once the loop is running, the spike goes nearly to ENa whatever started it, because the Na⁺ channels, not the stimulus, are now driving it. A stimulus either reaches threshold and gets the full spike, or doesn't and gets a small RC response that leaks away.

Show where the threshold is

m is fast, h and n are slow, so in the first moments after a push m has followed V but h and n are still at their resting values h0, n0. The total current is then a function of V alone:

\[ I_\mathrm{fast}(V) = \bar g_\mathrm{Na}\,m_\infty(V)^3 h_0\,(V - E_\mathrm{Na}) + \bar g_\mathrm{K}\,n_0^4\,(V - E_\mathrm{K}) + I_\mathrm{leak}(V) \]

It is zero at rest and outward just above it, so a small push is pushed back. Further up, the Na⁺ term takes over and the total crosses zero again, going inward: past that crossing the current pushes V on up. That second crossing is the threshold. The calculator below draws this curve. It slightly underestimates the real threshold, because h and n do start to move during the rise.

The same curve with h and n as a spike leaves them gives the threshold during recovery: smaller h0 shrinks the Na⁺ term, larger n0 grows the K⁺ term, and the second crossing moves up or disappears.

The K⁺ side of the tug of war is stronger: rest drops closer to EK, the leak drains the pulse's charge faster (τ is shorter) and threshold rises by about 6 mV, so the same pulse fails.

In plain words: Rest; a push past threshold; Na⁺ rushes in and the voltage shoots up; the Na⁺ channels plug themselves and K⁺ channels open, which brings the voltage back down, briefly below rest; then a short recovery, the refractory period, when firing again is harder or impossible.

1. Rest

Nearly all m gates are shut and most h gates are open, ready. The leaks hold V near −70 mV: mostly the K⁺ leak, pulling toward EK, with a little Na⁺ leak pulling it some 15 mV above.

2. The stimulus

The electrode's current charges the capacitor, as in lesson 2: V rises at I/C.

3. The rising phase

Past threshold, m opens within a fraction of a millisecond. gNa jumps a thousandfold, the Na⁺ current inward charges the capacitor, and V rushes toward ENa. It cannot pass ENa: there the Na⁺ driving force is zero.

4. The peak and the fall

Meanwhile the slow gates catch up. h closes, plugging the Na⁺ channels, and n opens, so K⁺ flows out. With Na⁺ off and K⁺ on, V falls back toward EK.

5. Recovery and the refractory period

Then n closes and h reopens. Until h has recovered, fewer Na⁺ channels are available and extra K⁺ channels are still open, so a second spike is hard or impossible: the refractory period.

In the threshold tug of war, both changes favour K⁺. With h low, the Na⁺ current at each voltage is smaller, so the dip in the current–voltage curve (the stretch where the Na⁺ current drags the total toward inward) is shallower. With n high, the K⁺ line is steeper and lifts the whole curve. So the threshold moves up, and a bigger stimulus is needed to fire (the relative refractory period). With fewer Na⁺ or more K⁺ channels than here the dip can fail to reach zero at all: no threshold, no spike whatever the stimulus (the absolute refractory period). And while the first spike is still above the new threshold, it is still running, so no second spike can start.

Because the Na⁺ leak holds rest a little above EK, V also dips below rest for a moment after the spike: the undershoot (afterhyperpolarization). While the K⁺ gated channels are still open, K⁺ dominates even more than at rest, and V is pulled closer to EK, here about 5 mV below rest, before n closes and the leaks bring it back. Set gNa,leak to 0 and rest drops to EK: then there is nothing lower to dip to, and the undershoot disappears.

Why the K⁺ gates matter

Without them, inactivation alone does not end the spike. A few Na⁺ channels stay open at depolarized voltages, in the window where m is open and h has not quite closed, and that small Na⁺ current outweighs the leaks: V sticks near −13 mV. The voltage-gated K⁺ channels supply the big outward current that brings V home, and the m-h-n timing is what makes the spike brief.

Notice how little charge all this takes. A 100 mV spike on 1 µF/cm² moves 100 nC/cm² of charge: a tiny fraction of the ions in the cell, so the concentrations, and the Nernst potentials, barely change.

In plain words: A voltage clamp is an amplifier that holds the membrane at a voltage you choose, however hard the channels try to move it. To hold it there it has to supply exactly the current the channels let through, so that current is something you can read off a meter. Holding V still is what let Hodgkin and Huxley see the Na⁺ and K⁺ channels one voltage at a time, and build the whole model from those recordings.

Why hold the voltage still?

The gates follow the voltage, and the voltage follows the currents through the gates. In a spike everything changes at once, so you cannot tell what the Na⁺ channels would do at, say, −20 mV: by the time you look, V has moved on. Clamp V at −20 mV and the loop is cut. The gates now settle toward their values for −20 mV, each at its own speed, and you can watch them do it.

How the amplifier does it

The amplifier has two wires into the cell. One measures V. The amplifier compares it with the command voltage VC you set: if V is too low, it pushes positive charge in through the second wire; if too high, it pulls charge out. It does this so fast and so hard that V barely moves from VC. It is a thermostat for voltage: measure, compare, correct, all the time.

Hodgkin and Huxley did it in the squid giant axon, about half a millimetre across: wide enough to slide a fine silver wire along the inside, so the whole length of membrane they measured was held at the same voltage.

What you measure

Current into the membrane goes two places: onto the capacitor (that is what changes V) or out through the channels. With V held still nothing more goes onto the capacitor, so everything the amplifier supplies is going through the channels. As in lesson 2, each kind of channel follows Ohm's law: current = conductance × driving force.

\[ I_\mathrm{amp} = \underbrace{C\,\frac{dV}{dt}}_{=\,0\ \text{when held}} + I_\mathrm{Na} + I_\mathrm{K} + I_\mathrm{leak} \]

The one exception is the instant of the step itself: moving V to its new value means putting charge on the capacitor, a brief spike of current that is over in microseconds. The simulation leaves it out.

Taking the current apart

Step from rest to 0 mV: the current flows in for a fraction of a millisecond, then turns out and stays out. Two currents are adding up. Hodgkin and Huxley separated them by replacing most of the Na⁺ outside the axon with a larger ion that cannot pass through the channels: the early inward current shrank, so it is Na⁺, and what was left was K⁺. Today the same split is done with toxins: tetrodotoxin (TTX, from pufferfish) blocks the Na⁺ channels, tetraethylammonium (TEA) the K⁺ channels.

From currents to gates

Divide each current by its driving force and you have the conductance, g = I / (V − E). Do it at many command voltages and two patterns appear. gNa switches on fast and then switches itself off even though V is still held (m opens, then h closes). gK switches on more slowly and stays on as long as V is held (n). How fast each rises, and where it ends up at each voltage, gave Hodgkin and Huxley every gate's τ and x∞.

Why a held voltage makes the gates simple

Each gate obeys

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{x_\infty(V) - x}{\tau_x(V)} \]

With V clamped, x∞ and τx are just numbers, so each gate relaxes along a single exponential from where it started to x∞:

\[ x(t) = x_\infty - (x_\infty - x_0)\,e^{-t/\tau_x} \]

Fitting gK = ḡKn⁴ and gNa = ḡNam³h to the recorded conductances gives x∞ and τx at each voltage. The powers (n⁴, m³) are what give the curves the slight delay at their start that Hodgkin and Huxley saw.

Then they put the pieces back together, let V go free, and solved the equations by hand on a mechanical calculator. Out came an action potential with the right shape, threshold and speed, built from currents measured while nothing was allowed to spike.

Voltage clamp and current clamp

The clamp simulation above does both. Current clamp is what an electrode in a living cell usually does: inject a current you choose and watch V do what it likes; that is how you see spikes. Voltage clamp turns it round: you choose V and watch the current; that is how you see the channels.

  • Currents: what the clamp amplifier records after the step at t = 0, outward positive: INa (inward, early), IK (outward, late) and their sum (black), the trace Hodgkin and Huxley actually saw. The capacitor's brief charging current at the step, and the small leak current, are left out, as they subtracted them.
  • Conductances: each current divided by its driving force, g = I / (V − E). gNa rises and falls back with V held still; gK rises and stays.
  • Gates: m, h and n relaxing from their values at the holding voltage to their values at the step voltage, each with its own τ.
  • Peak current vs voltage: repeat the step to every voltage. Orange: the largest Na⁺ current; purple: the K⁺ current after 5 ms. The Na⁺ curve is U-shaped: small steps open few m gates, and near ENa there is no driving force. It reverses sign at ENa. The dot is the step set above.
