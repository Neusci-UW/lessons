Electrical synapses and presynaptic inhibition

Two more ways neurons pass signals, each built from pieces you already know. At an electrical synapse, channels called gap junctions join the inside of one cell to the inside of the next. Current flows straight through them, as through a resistor, in either direction, with no transmitter and no delay. In presynaptic inhibition, a synapse onto another neuron’s axon terminal turns that terminal’s release down before it happens: the spike in the terminal is smaller, so less Ca²⁺ comes in, and because release follows Ca²⁺ so steeply (The Chemical Synapse lesson), a small cut in Ca²⁺ is a big cut in release.

You'll be able to
  • treat a gap junction as a resistor between two cells, and work out the coupling coefficient with Ohm’s law, gc/(gc + gin)
  • explain why electrical synapses are fast and two-way, why they pass slow changes better than spikes, and how they pull cells into firing together
  • explain presynaptic inhibition: Cl⁻ channels opened on a terminal shunt the arriving spike and inactivate some of its Na⁺ channels, so less Ca²⁺ enters
  • use release ∝ (Ca²⁺ entry)4 to predict how much a small cut in Ca²⁺ entry cuts release, for presynaptic inhibition, autoreceptors and drugs that act on terminals

Simulation

Start here
  1. Give cell 1 a small negative current step. Cell 2 follows, by less. The ratio of the two changes is the coupling coefficient: compare it with Ohm’s law.
  2. Step cell 2 instead: cell 1 follows by the same fraction. This junction conducts both ways.
  3. Make cell 1 fire. The slow part of the step crosses well, but each spike reaches cell 2 only as a small, rounded bump, a spikelet.
  4. Two cells driven to fire at different rates, with the junction shut. After half a second it opens wide, and they fire together.
  5. In Part 2, a GABAA synapse on the terminal is open when the spike arrives. The spike there shrinks, Ca²⁺ entry falls by about a third, and release by three quarters.
  6. The terminal’s own transmitter, acting on its metabotropic receptors, has closed a third of its Ca²⁺ channels: release falls to a fifth.

Theory

In plain words: A gap junction is a patch of channels that joins the inside of one cell to the inside of the next. To the current it is just a resistor between the two cells: current flows through it from the cell at the higher voltage to the cell at the lower, in either direction, as Ohm’s law says.

Channels that join two cells

Each cell builds half a channel, a connexon: a ring of six connexin proteins. Where two cells come within a few nanometres of each other, their connexons line up and dock, making one pore that runs from cytoplasm to cytoplasm. It is wide enough for ions and small molecules such as ATP, cAMP and IP3. Hundreds of these channels cluster in a patch, the gap junction.

Different cells use different connexins. Neurons use mainly connexin 36. Heart muscle uses connexin 43, which is how each beat spreads through the whole heart. Mutations in the connexin of Schwann cells (connexin 32) cause an inherited neuropathy, X-linked Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, and mutations in connexin 26 of the inner ear are a common cause of inherited deafness.

Ohm’s law across the junction

All the channels together are one conductance, gc (c for coupling), between the two insides. The current through it is the conductance times the voltage difference across it, the same Ohm’s law as for any channel, with the other cell’s voltage in place of a battery:

\[ I_\mathrm{gap} = g_c\,(V_1 - V_2) \]

For example, with cell 1 at −85 mV, cell 2 at −75 mV and gc = 0.05 mS/cm², Igap = 0.05 × (−10) = −0.5 µA/cm²: the current flows from cell 2 into cell 1, pulling cell 2 down and cell 1 up.

The coupling coefficient

Pass a steady current into cell 1 and wait until the voltages stop changing. All the current that reaches cell 2 through the junction leaves through cell 2’s own membrane, whose conductance is its input conductance gin. The fraction of cell 1’s change that cell 2 sees is the coupling coefficient:

\[ CC = \frac{\Delta V_2}{\Delta V_1} = \frac{g_c}{g_c + g_\mathrm{in}} \]

This is a voltage divider: the junction and cell 2’s membrane are two resistors in series between cell 1 and the outside, and cell 2 sits in between. With gc = 0.05 and gin = 0.1 mS/cm² (the simulation’s leak), CC = 0.05 / 0.15 = 0.33: when cell 1 moves 15 mV, cell 2 moves 5 mV. A wide-open junction makes CC approach 1, but never reach it. Between neurons in the brain the coupling is usually weak, often a few per cent.

Show the derivation

Once the voltages are steady, cell 2’s capacitor carries no current. So the current in through the junction equals the current out through cell 2’s membrane:

\[ g_c\,(\Delta V_1 - \Delta V_2) = g_\mathrm{in}\,\Delta V_2 \]

Collect the terms with ΔV2:

\[ \Delta V_2\,(g_c + g_\mathrm{in}) = g_c\,\Delta V_1 \]

and divide by ΔV1(gc + gin). The size of the step drops out, so CC is a property of the junction and of the cell receiving the signal. That is why it is not always the same both ways: from a large cell (large gin) to a small one the coupling is strong, from the small cell to the large one weak, even through the same junction. The two cells here are identical, so CC is the same both ways.

Fast and two-way

Nothing has to be released, cross a gap and bind, so there is no synaptic delay: cell 2 starts to change the moment cell 1 does. Escape circuits use this, from the crayfish’s tail flip, where Furshpan and Potter found the first electrical synapse, to the goldfish’s Mauthner cell. Most gap junctions conduct equally both ways. The crayfish’s does not: it is a rectifier, passing current from the giant fibre to the motor neuron but hardly back.

In plain words: Cell 2’s capacitor has to be charged through the junction. A slow change has time to charge it; a spike, over in a millisecond, does not. So slow changes cross the junction well and spikes arrive as small, rounded bumps. And two coupled cells tug on each other’s timing until they fire together.

A low-pass filter

The junction and cell 2’s membrane are an RC circuit, as in Passive Properties: Time. For a quick change, the capacitor acts like one more conductance beside cell 2’s leak, of about C divided by how long the change lasts. For a spike lasting about 1 ms, that is 1 µF/cm² ÷ 1 ms = 1 mS/cm², ten times the leak. Put it in the voltage divider:

\[ CC_\text{fast} \approx \frac{g_c}{g_c + g_\mathrm{in} + C/\Delta t} \]

With gc = 0.03 mS/cm² (step 3 of Start here), the slow part of a step crosses at 0.03 / 0.13 = 0.23, but a spike at about 0.03 / 1.13 ≈ 0.03: a 100 mV spike makes a bump of 2 to 3 mV. That bump is the spikelet, the sign of an electrical synapse in a recording.

Show where C/Δt comes from

The capacitor’s current is C dV/dt. A change ΔV made over a time Δt needs a current of about C ΔV/Δt: the same as the current through a conductance C/Δt with ΔV across it. So, for that change, the capacitor behaves like a conductance C/Δt, large for a brief change and small for a slow one. For a slow change it vanishes and the formula is the steady coupling coefficient again.

The same circuit gives cell 2’s time constant, the capacitor charged through the junction and its leak in parallel: τ = C/(gc + gin) = 1 / 0.13 ≈ 8 ms. A spike is far shorter than that, so cell 2 hardly starts to charge before the spike is over.

Firing together

When the faster cell fires, its spike pushes current through the junction into the slower cell, nudging it toward threshold so that it fires a little sooner. Between spikes, the slower cell sits lower and holds the faster one back. Spike by spike, each cell pulls the other’s timing toward its own, until they fire together. With weak coupling the two cells still settle at the same rate, but with the faster one a little ahead.

In the cortex, fast-spiking inhibitory interneurons are joined to one another by connexin 36 junctions, and networks of them fire in synchrony; in mice without connexin 36 that synchrony is largely lost. Synchrony of inhibitory cells helps produce the brain’s fast rhythms. Gap junctions also couple cells in the retina and in the inferior olive, and they let heart and gut muscle contract as one.

In plain words: A synapse onto an axon terminal can turn that terminal’s release down before it happens. The Cl⁻ channels it opens leak away the current that the arriving spike uses to charge the terminal, and the slight depolarization they cause inactivates some of the terminal’s Na⁺ channels. The spike in the terminal is smaller, fewer Ca²⁺ channels open, and because release follows Ca²⁺ so steeply, release falls a lot.

Where it happens

The best-known case is in the spinal cord. Interneurons make GABA synapses on the terminals of sensory axons, such as the stretch-receptor axons that excite motor neurons in the knee-jerk reflex. These terminals keep Cl⁻ high inside, so ECl sits above their resting voltage. When the GABAA channels open, Cl⁻ flows out and the terminal depolarizes by a few mV: primary afferent depolarization, or PAD. Eccles and colleagues found that the reflex was inhibited for as long as the sensory terminals stayed depolarized, while the motor neuron itself showed no IPSP.

Ohm’s law at the terminal

The spike in the axon charges the terminal through the branch leading into it, a conductance gax. At each moment the terminal settles where its currents balance, so it is a weighted average of everything pulling on it, the same formula as the resting voltage with several batteries (the Membrane Potential lesson):

\[ V_\text{term} \approx \frac{g_\mathrm{ax}V_\text{axon} + g_L E_L + g_\mathrm{GABA}E_\mathrm{Cl}}{g_\mathrm{ax} + g_L + g_\mathrm{GABA}} \]

At the spike’s peak the axon is at +40 mV. With the simulation’s gax = 1.5 and gL = 0.3 mS/cm² and no GABA, the terminal would reach (1.5 × 40 − 0.3 × 70) / 1.8 = +22 mV. Open 1.2 mS/cm² of GABAA channels with ECl = −55 mV and it reaches only (60 − 21 − 66) / 3.0 = −9 mV. The terminal’s own Na⁺ channels then boost both, to +35 and +18 mV in the simulation. The shunt does not need ECl to be below rest; it works by adding a conductance, not by pulling down.

Show the derivation, and the depolarization before the spike

With the capacitor’s current left out (the terminal is small and charges fast), the currents into the terminal add to zero:

\[ g_\mathrm{ax}(V_\text{axon} - V) + g_L(E_L - V) + g_\mathrm{GABA}(E_\mathrm{Cl} - V) = 0 \]

Collect the terms with V and divide by gax + gL + gGABA to get the formula above. Before the spike the axon is at rest, Vaxon = EL = −70 mV, and the formula gives the PAD:

\[ V_\text{before} = E_L + \frac{g_\mathrm{GABA}\,(E_\mathrm{Cl} - E_L)}{g_\mathrm{ax} + g_L + g_\mathrm{GABA}} = -70 + \frac{1.2 \times 15}{3.0} = -64\ \mathrm{mV} \]

Six millivolts is enough to inactivate about a quarter of the terminal’s Na⁺ channels: in the simulation 60% are available at −64 mV against 83% at −70 mV. With ECl above rest the inhibition is stronger than the shunt alone, as Graham and Redman found in a detailed model of a terminal.

Why a small cut in Ca²⁺ is a big cut in release

Ca²⁺ channels open steeply with voltage, so a spike 17 mV lower lets in about 30% less Ca²⁺. And a vesicle fuses only when several Ca²⁺ ions, about four, are bound to its sensor at once (The Chemical Synapse), so release goes roughly as the fourth power of the Ca²⁺:

\[ \text{release} \propto \left(\text{Ca}^{2+}\ \text{entry}\right)^4: \qquad 0.7^4 \approx 0.24 \]

Measured against the Ca²⁺ that actually enters a terminal, the power is between about 2.5 and 4 depending on the synapse and how the Ca²⁺ is cut; this lesson uses 4. Try other powers in the calculator below.

One input at a time

An IPSP on the soma or a dendrite (postsynaptic inhibition) makes the whole cell less likely to fire, whatever excites it. Presynaptic inhibition turns down one input and leaves the cell’s other inputs alone. The spinal cord uses it to adjust how much each kind of sensory input gets through, for example during movement.

In plain words: Terminals also carry metabotropic receptors. When they bind transmitter, whether the terminal’s own (autoreceptors) or another cell’s, a G protein closes some of the terminal’s Ca²⁺ channels. The spike is unchanged, but fewer channels let in less Ca²⁺, and release falls far more.

G-protein βγ subunits close Ca²⁺ channels

A metabotropic receptor activates a G protein, which splits into an α subunit and a βγ pair. The βγ pair binds the terminal’s Ca²⁺ channels (the N and P/Q types that trigger release) and makes them much harder to open. With a third of the channels out of action, the same spike lets in two thirds of the Ca²⁺:

\[ \left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^4 \approx 0.20 \]

so release falls to a fifth. Close half the channels and release falls to 0.54 ≈ 6%.

Autoreceptors: a terminal listening to itself

Many terminals have receptors for their own transmitter, which turn release down when a lot has been released: negative feedback. Noradrenaline acts on α2 receptors on noradrenergic terminals, dopamine on D2 receptors on dopaminergic ones, and GABA on GABAB receptors on GABAergic ones.

Receptors for other cells’ transmitters, and drugs

Terminals also carry receptors for other cells’ transmitters. In the spinal cord, the terminals of pain fibres have μ-opioid receptors: the body’s own enkephalins, and morphine and other opioids, cut the release of glutamate and substance P from them, one of the ways opioids relieve pain. Baclofen, used for muscle spasticity, acts on GABAB receptors, including those on the terminals of sensory axons in the spinal cord. And many neurons release endocannabinoids that travel backward to CB1 receptors on the terminals that excite them.
